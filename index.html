The Crow’s One Job—and the Feast That Ruined It 🐦‍⬛

Noah sent a crow to look for dry land. The bird found something else first, and the Ark never got its answer.

Picture the view from the Ark: water where the roads, roofs, and fields used to be. Everyone aboard was waiting on the same question. Had the flood begun to fall?

Noah chose the crow as his scout. It was strong enough to cross the drowned world, spot any sign of land, and fly back with the news. One job—out and back.

The crow took off over the gray water. Then it noticed a carcass floating among the wreckage. Duty lost that argument quickly.

Down the bird went. It settled beside the carrion and started eating. One bite led to the next, while the Ark, the waiting animals, and the promised message faded from its mind.

Noah kept watching the horizon. An hour passed. Then more time. The crow had found a meal, but it never came back.

I find that detail more biting than any grand disaster. The storm did not beat the crow, and weak wings did not fail it. A reward sitting right in front of its beak mattered more than the people depending on its return.

So the bird is remembered not for the distance it flew, but for the message it never delivered. That is the sting in this old tale: leaving is easy. A trusted messenger proves itself by coming back.

The Fox Came Back for Seconds 🦊🍗

Stealing one chicken from Imam Al-Shafi'i’s camp was bold. Returning for the replacement was almost cheeky.

Dinner was supposed to be simple. A chicken had been prepared for Imam Al-Shafi'i, everyone was settling in, and somewhere just beyond the camp a fox was paying very close attention.

The animal waited for that tiny gap when every human eye was elsewhere. Then—straight in, chicken in its jaws, gone into the fading light before anyone could react.

Naturally, the camp erupted. They prepared another chicken, now fully aware that a fox was nearby. You would think that ended the problem. Surely nobody pulls the same theft twice against people who are waiting for it.

This fox apparently took that as a challenge.

It circled back, watched the newly alert camp, found another opening, and slipped away with the replacement too. I can almost imagine the silence after everyone realized what had happened.

By then it was no longer a story about hunger. It was about nerve. The people adjusted after the first raid, and the fox adjusted right along with them.

That is why foxes keep winning space in old animal literature. They are rarely the strongest creature in the scene. Timing, patience, and the willingness to try a door everyone assumes is closed—that is their real weapon.

Two chickens gone. One camp thoroughly embarrassed. And one useful warning left behind: if a fox succeeds once, do not assume it has finished.

Source: Al-Damiri, Hayat al-Hayawan al-Kubra.

A Wolf Starts This 700-Year-Old Chain. A Fly Ends It. ⛓️🐺

The old account follows ten creatures from a formidable hunter all the way down to the smallest mouth in the sequence.

It begins with a wolf, which feels like the obvious place for danger to peak: speed, teeth, and a body made for the chase. But Hayat al-Hayawan refuses to stop there. It keeps asking, “And what did that animal eat?”

The answer stretches into a chain of ten creatures. One hunter catches smaller prey; that creature has fed on something below it. The scale keeps shrinking—from jaws to smaller mouths, from heavy bodies to quick lives tucked into grass, fur, feathers, and dust.

Here is the part I love: every new link changes the animal before it. A frightening predator becomes somebody else’s meal. “Hunter” and “prey” turn out to be temporary roles, not permanent titles.

By the final links, the wolf feels almost like a distant mountain. Yet the last insect matters just as much as the creature at the top. Take it away and the whole odd procession stops connecting.

This is not a modern classroom chart with tidy arrows and scientific labels. It is a medieval observation told as a verbal spectacle—ten lives joined by appetite, centuries before “food chain” became an everyday phrase.

The surprise is not that animals eat one another. It is how relentlessly the account zooms in. Strength gives way to vulnerability; size gives way to detail.

The wolf opens the chain, but it never owns it. Even the tiniest fly gets the last word in a sequence that began with a howl.

Source: Al-Damiri, Hayat al-Hayawan al-Kubra.

The Sneeze That Put a Cat on Noah’s Ark 🦁🐱

The animals first needed protection from the lion. Then the mice took over. The old tale solves both problems with one enormous sneeze.

Noah’s Ark was full of animals that would never willingly sleep side by side. Hooves beside claws. Wings beside teeth. Now put a lion among them and try to relax.

The other creatures could not. Even resting, the lion was powerful enough to terrify everything nearby.

The medieval account says GOD struck the lion with a fever—the first fever on Earth. The heat weakened the great animal, and the panic aboard the Ark finally eased. The king of beasts, the book says, remained feverish from then onward.

Problem solved? Not quite.

Mice began multiplying inside the vessel. They chewed through food and goods, treating the carefully packed stores like an endless banquet. The animals were safer from the lion, but now tiny destroyers were eating their supplies.

And the answer came from the animal everyone had feared.

The lion’s fever rose. Its nose twitched, its chest tightened, and then came a sneeze big enough for an old animal legend.

Out popped a cat.

Small, quick, and made for the Ark’s narrow spaces, it chased the mice into hiding and protected the stores. I had to read that line twice the first time I met it.

That reversal is the whole charm of the story. The lion begins as the danger and ends as the source of help. The cat does not arrive with a roar—just one impossible burst of breath, ready to follow wherever the mice run.

Source: Al-Damiri, Hayat al-Hayawan al-Kubra.

The Lion: 630 Names, a Sneeze, and the Birth of the Cat 🦁

Quick Answer

What is the strangest lion belief in Hayat al-Hayawan?

The strangest lion belief in Hayat al-Hayawan is the Ark story in which GOD gives the lion a fever, then makes it sneeze out the first cat when mice threaten the food stores. The entry also records 630 lion names, unusual habits, historical stories, and medieval remedies.

At a glance

  • The entry records 630 names and attributes for the lion.
  • The Ark story links the lion’s sneeze to the first cat.
  • Its remedies and dream meanings are presented as medieval beliefs.

Names, nature and the lion’s code

The medieval Arabic encyclopedia Hayat al-Hayawan (c. 1371) opens with the lion — "because it is the noblest of wild animals." Ibn Khalawayh collected 500 names for it; another linguist added 130 more: Usama, Haydara, al-Layth, al-Ghadanfar, al-Qaswara — and kunyas like Abu al-Harith (father of plowers). By the lion's nobility, Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib was named Asadullah — the Lion of GOD.

The entry is full of wonders. The lioness gives birth to a single cub — a lump of flesh with no sensation. She guards it for three days while the father breathes into it again and again, until it stirs. Its eyes open after seven days, and after six months it hunts on its own.

The lion lives by a strict honor code: it never eats another predator's kill, it abandons its prey once full and never returns to it — it tears its food in chunks without chewing, and having almost no saliva, it is proverbially foul-breathed ("more stinking of breath than the lion," the Arabs said, alongside "braver than the lion"). And yet, the book claims, this king of beasts flees at the crow of a rooster, is bewildered by fire, is frightened by the clatter of a copper basin — and by cats. The cat. Remember that for later.

The stories in this entry could fill a book on their own. Safina, the Prophet's freedman, was once shipwrecked and met a lion in a thicket. He introduced himself — "I am the freedman of the Messenger of GOD, and I am lost" — and the lion nudged him with its shoulder until it had set him on the road, then hummed what Safina took for a farewell.

Stories, wonders and medieval beliefs

Then there's the fate of 'Utba ibn Abi Lahab, whom the Prophet cursed: "O GOD, set upon him one of Your dogs." A lion tore him apart in Syria — sniffing past the ring of guards, leaping onto the piled baggage where 'Utba slept, and tearing his head off. They searched for the lion afterward and never found it.

And Daniel in the lions' pit: the lions licked him and wagged their tails, and an angel brought him food. Centuries later, his signet ring was found engraved with two lions licking a man between them — made as a reminder of GOD's favor.

Then comes the strangest tale of all. Aboard Noah's Ark, the animals feared the lion, so GOD struck it with a fever — the FIRST fever ever on Earth, the book says. When mice began ruining the food stores, GOD made the lion SNEEZE — and out of that mighty sneeze came the CAT, and the mouse hid from it forever. Remember the lion's fear of cats? The book set that up pages before the punchline.

The khawass — the "special properties" — are a pharmacy of wonders, all presented as the book's claims, never as advice: lion fat smeared on the body makes predators flee; a piece of lion skin hung on a child cures epilepsy; carrying a lion's tooth protects from toothache. And the dream-book: a lion in dreams is a mighty, oppressive sultan — but fleeing from one, you escape what you fear and attain wisdom.

Even the lion's roar, the book reports, carries a prayer: "O GOD, do not give me power over anyone who does good."

The Camel That Blows a Red Balloon Out of Its Mouth 🐫

Quick Answer

Why is the camel called one of the most amazing animals in the book?

Hayat al-Hayawan marvels at the camel as a familiar giant built for desert travel: it kneels for loads, eats thorny plants, carries a household, and is said to endure ten days without water. Its strangest detail is the male’s red inflatable throat sac, the shaqshaqah, displayed during mating season.

At a glance

  • The book calls camels “ships of the land.”
  • It says the camel has no gallbladder and can digest thorns.
  • The male displays a red throat sac called the shaqshaqah.

Desert design and language

The medieval Arabic encyclopedia Hayat al-Hayawan (c. 1371) calls the camel one of the most amazing animals — not because it is rare, but because we see it so often that we stopped noticing. A giant that kneels to take its load, can be led by a mouse pulling its bridle, and carries a whole furnished house on its back — food, drink, clothes, and a roof — while it walks. The book says GOD made camels long-necked "ships of the land," built to go ten days without water, and paired them with sailing ships in the Quran: "…and on them and on ships you are carried."

The entry is a treasure chest of linguistic precision. "Al-ibl" isn't even a plural, grammatically — it's a genus-name, and feminine. The diminutive is ubaylah, "little camels." The camel's kunya, its nickname, is Abu Ayyub. And camels are called banat al-layl — "daughters of the night." A sage raised in a land with no camels once reasoned from first principles: "they are probably long-necked, to help them lift heavy loads" — the book presents this as proof of the design.

Here's a marvel I didn't expect: the camel is the only animal with no gallbladder. The book states it flatly — every animal has one except the camel — and links it to the camel's proverbial patience and endurance. On its liver sits a membrane holding a secretion used as kohl, said to help chronic night-blindness. It relishes thorny trees, its gut digesting what would shred other stomachs — yet it often cannot digest barley. The desert's paradox, chewing thorns.

The red display and other medieval claims

But the strangest detail comes during mating season. The male camel's temper sours, he eats less and carries on anyway, loaded with three times his normal load. Then he pushes out the shaqshaqah: a red flap of skin from deep inside his throat that he inflates like a balloon from his cheek. "No one knows what it is," the author marvels. The stallion mates only once a year, but for a long season that leaves him exhausted — and the shaqshaqah became proverbial for violent outbursts: the Prophet himself warned about a man's shaqshaqahs, comparing his rages to the bellowing stallion.

The book also calls the camel "the most spiteful of animals" — and the most modest. It claims no camel will ever mount its own mother: one was tricked into it by a man who covered the female with a cloth; when the son realized the truth, he cut off his own organ and held a grudge until he killed the man. Another, tricked the same way, killed himself.

The breeds get their own catalog — al-Arhabiyya, al-Mahriyya, al-Majdiya of Yemen — and the book even corrects al-Ghazali, who wrongly said "Mahriyya" meant inferior camels. The epithets are poetry: al-'is (strong and hardy), al-najiyah (swift), al-kawma' (huge-humped). And the proverbs! "This is not how you water camels, Sa'd" — of one who botches a task he's unfit for.

Some entries are medieval beliefs, not facts. The book claims a camel that catches sight of the star Canopus drops dead on the spot, that burnt camel hair stops bleeding, and that camel urine instantly sobers a drunk man. And in a 7th-century glimpse of heredity: when a man wondered how his red camels produced a grey offspring, the answer given was "perhaps an old root pulled it" — a trait resurfacing from generations back.

    The book calls camels “ships of the land” and says they were built to travel for ten days without water. It also emphasizes their ability to kneel for heavy loads, carry food, drink, clothing and shelter, and eat thorny plants that would damage the stomachs of other animals.

  3. Are all the camel claims in the article modern facts?

    No. The article clearly separates the source’s medieval beliefs from advice or modern zoology. Claims about Canopus killing a camel, burnt camel hair stopping bleeding, and camel urine sobering a drunk person are reported as historical curiosities from Hayat al-Hayawan, not recommendations or verified medical facts.

The Mystery Birds With Trunks Like Elephants and Paws Like Dogs 🐦

Quick Answer

What did medieval scholars think the Ababil birds looked like?

Hayat al-Hayawan preserves several competing descriptions of the Ababil: birds with dog-like paws, green sea birds with lion-like heads, bat-like birds, starlings, or swallows. The entry does not settle the question; instead, it records how scholars, lexicographers, and witnesses understood the mysterious Quranic flock.

At a glance

  • Five different identifications are preserved in the entry.
  • Some scholars linked the Ababil with swallows at the Sacred Mosque.
  • The word’s possible singular forms were disputed.

Five descriptions of the mysterious flock

The Quran tells of an army with elephants marching to destroy the Kaaba — and of "طيراً أبابيل," birds in flocks sent against them. But what were these birds? The 700-year-old Hayat al-Hayawan collects the scholars' answers, and they read like five different animals.

Sa'id ibn Jubayr said they were birds that nest between heaven and earth, with trunks like birds' trunks and paws like dogs' paws. Ikrimah described green birds that rose out of the sea, with heads like lions' heads. Ibn Abbas said they were like the balsan plant — or like bats. Ubadah ibn al-Samit thought they were starlings. And Aisha said they most resembled swallows — adding a detail that still gives me goosebumps: the book promises, in a later chapter, that these are the swallows (السنونو, singular sununah) that nest in the Sacred Mosque to this day. The miracle birds, the book suggests, never left. They're still there, in Mecca, right now.

A word with no agreed singular

What fascinates me about this entry is that the scholars disagreed about everything, including the grammar. Even the word itself puzzled the lexicographers: they could not agree on a singular. Was it ubalah, ubbul, ibbil, or ibal? Abu Ubayd threw up his hands: the word simply has no singular from its own form. But the debate didn't stop there — al-Farisi reported that he had personally heard someone say "ubballah" (with a doubled consonant) as the singular, while al-Farra' narrated "iballah" in a lightened form. Two more candidates, both from direct testimony. Six possible singulars for one plural, and the great Abu Ubayd insisting there is none at all. What I love about the medieval scholars is that they would argue about a single word with the intensity we reserve for politics.

The same root wanders into unexpected places. Al-abil also meant a Christian monk — and the entry preserves the actual poetry to prove it, verses ending "...Abil al-Abiliyyin, 'Isa ibn Maryam" — "the Abil of Abils, Jesus son of Mary." The Arabs called Jesus himself "Abil al-Abiliyyin," the monk of monks, in an old battle poem. And ibalah (with a kasra) is a bundle of firewood, giving Arabic the proverb "a bundle upon a bundle" — calamity piled on calamity.

One thing the entry notably does not say: anything about the stones the birds carried. No mention of what they were made of, no details at all. The miracle is in the birds themselves: their identity, their strangeness, their possible survival into the present day.

Frequently Asked Questions

  1. What does Hayat al-Hayawan say the Ababil were?

    The book records several answers rather than choosing one. The Ababil were described as birds with dog-like paws, green birds rising from the sea with lion-like heads, creatures like bats or the balsan plant, starlings, and swallows. The disagreement is part of what makes the short entry memorable.

  2. Does the entry identify the Ababil as swallows?

    Aisha is reported as saying the birds most resembled swallows. The article adds that Hayat al-Hayawan later connects them with swallows nesting in the Sacred Mosque. That is one preserved interpretation among several, not a conclusion accepted by every scholar quoted in the entry.

  3. Why did scholars argue about the word Ababil?

    Lexicographers could not agree on a singular form for Ababil. Proposed forms included ubalah, ubbul, ibbil, ibal, ubballah and iballah, while Abu Ubayd said the word had no singular from its own form. The debate shows how closely medieval scholars examined the language surrounding the Quranic account.

The She-Donkey That Cures Pride 🫏

Quick Answer

How does the she-donkey become a lesson in humility?

In Hayat al-Hayawan, riding a she-donkey is included among actions that push pride from the heart, alongside wearing wool, serving one’s own animals, sitting with poor believers, and eating with family. A related Bedouin story turns the humble mount into a symbol of dignity stronger than wealth or office.

At a glance

  • The she-donkey is linked with humility in a reported saying.
  • A Bedouin on a lean donkey refuses a governor’s money.
  • Dream traditions treat the animal as a sign of help or provision.

Humility, language and a desert encounter

Of all the animals in Hayat al-Hayawan, the humble she-donkey — al-atan — gets one of the strangest honors: the book records a Prophetic saying that riding a she-donkey drives pride out of the heart. "Whoever wears wool, milks his sheep, and rides the she-donkey — there is no arrogance left inside him." A second tradition, reported through Jabir and Abu Hurayra, lists the cures for pride in full: wearing wool, sitting with poor believers, riding the donkey, tethering the goat with one's own hands, and eating with one's family. Humility, it turns out, is a to-do list — and the donkey is on it.

Before we go further, a lexicographic footnote the book insists on: don't say atanah — that's wrong. The plural for a few is atn, and for many, utun. And there's a verb for it too: ista'tana al-rajul — a man "took a she-donkey for himself." The language loved this animal enough to mint verbs for owning her.

The book illustrates the pride-cure with a desert encounter I can't stop thinking about. Khalid ibn Abdallah al-Qasri, the governor of Iraq, was out hunting when he got separated from his companions — and met a Bedouin riding a lean, worn-out she-donkey, an old woman behind him. The man launched into magnificent tribal boasting and recited verses begging for generosity after "the succession of bad years."

Dreams, dignity and the meaning of atan

Then came the catch. Khalid — still incognito — pointed out the contradiction: "You came on a lean she-donkey, yet your poem claims you came on fine camels!" His reply is one of the great lines in the book: "Our toiling in praising the mean man was harsher upon us than lying in our poetry." Flattering a worthless man, he's saying, cost more dignity than inventing verses ever could.

Then the Bedouin learned the stranger was Khalid himself. The governor offered him money — "as a gift, not a reward." And the Bedouin refused with an oath that still rings: "By GOD, I will never take a dirham from a man after making him hear what he dislikes" — then turned his she-donkey around and rode off. "It is by such deeds," Khalid said, watching him go, "that he and his fathers earned what they earned." The man with nothing, on the leanest mount in the desert, out-dignified the governor.

Dreams about the she-donkey run darker. Zaraara ibn Amr al-Nakha'i came to the Prophet in the ninth year with a dream: his she-donkey bore a dark-speckled kid, while fire burst from the earth crying "Blaze! Blaze!" The interpretation unfolded piece by piece. The kid? A slave woman he'd left behind had borne him a boy. Why dark-speckled? The Prophet asked him: "Is there vitiligo upon you that you conceal?" — "By Him who sent you with the truth, no one knew of it before you." And the fire was the civil strife to come: people would kill their leader and clash "like the interlocking of the skull's plates" — the Prophet interlaced his fingers to show it. Scholars later identified it with the turmoil in which the caliph Uthman was killed. Zaraara begged for a prayer not to live to see it, and the Prophet prayed for him.

Arabic even turned the animal into an insult for fallen pride: "He was a donkey, then became a she-donkey" — said of a man humbled after glory. Yet the dream-books call her a fortunate sign: a helpful woman, steady profit, offspring — for atan comes from ityan, "coming": good things coming your way.

Together, these ordinary acts are presented as ways to remove arrogance by bringing a person closer to service and everyday life.

  3. What does a she-donkey mean in the article’s dream tradition?

    The dream material gives the she-donkey a more favorable meaning than some of the entry’s insults and omens. It can represent a helpful woman, steady profit, or offspring. The article links this reading to the word atan and the root meaning “coming,” suggesting good things arriving.

The Bird That's Also a Donkey?! 🐦🫏

Quick Answer

How can al-akhtab mean both a bird and a donkey?

Al-akhtab is a shared Arabic name recorded for two animals: a shrike-like calling bird and a donkey marked with green or a dark stripe along its back. Hayat al-Hayawan keeps both definitions, using poetry and grammatical authority to show how one word carried separate images in Arabic tradition.

At a glance

  • One definition identifies al-akhtab as a shrike.
  • Another applies it to a striped donkey.
  • A poem preserves the bird’s role in omen-reading.

The calling bird and its omen

Medieval Arabic lexicographers loved a good puzzle, and al-akhtab is one of their best: the same word names two completely different animals.

The entry opens with a morphological note that tells you everything about how the Arabic lexicographers thought: the word follows the pattern of al-ahmar — the af'al pattern, the pattern of colors and characteristics. Then comes the first definition: "it is said it is the shrike" (al-surad) — a sharp-beaked songbird.

An old poet swore he would never let a bird-omen turn him from his resolve, and the book preserves his exact words: "I will not turn back from an omen... when the akhtab, the caller, chirps from the tall branches." Notice the details the entry bothers to record: the bird is al-da'i, the caller — its identity is its cry. Its perch is al-dawh, the high branches. Its sound is sarsara, a chirping. The verse belongs to the old Arab science of tiyarah — reading the future in birds' flight and cries — where a single chirp from the high branches could cancel a journey. The poet's boast is that not even the calling akhtab will stop him.

The striped donkey and a dictionary trial

But turn the page and the akhtab becomes a donkey — one whose back is covered with greenness, the green overlying its spine like a blanket. And the great grammarian al-Farra' was even more precise with his wording: al-khatba' is the she-donkey with a black line running down her back — and the male of that marking is akhtab. The masculine form matches our entry word exactly. So the same three syllables hold a striped she-donkey and her male counterpart.

So which is it — songbird or striped donkey? The 700-year-old Hayat al-Hayawan records both without blinking, the way a field guide might list two species sharing one name. For the Arabs, a word could hold a whole menagerie: a cry in the treetops and a stripe on a donkey's spine, folded into three syllables.

What I love about this entry is what it reveals about the Arabic dictionary tradition. The lexicographers weren't just defining words — they were adjudicating between authorities. The animal entry is really a courtroom, and the word is on trial.

A she-donkey with a black line down her back, standing in some desert long ago, giving her name to a grammatical pattern. The dictionary as literature — only in Hayat al-Hayawan.

A green bird and a dark reputation

The akhayl is a small mystery in green. Hayat al-Hayawan describes it as a green bird with flashes on its wings of a different color — the Arabic is precise about this: "flashes differing from its color." And it earned its name from the khilan, the speckles scattered across it, like beauty marks on a face.

Some identified it as the roller (al-shaqraq). But the entry never describes the roller itself — it just points forward, saying the bird "will come under the chapter of the dotted shin" — its own entry appears later in the book under the letter ش. The medieval encyclopedia as a web of cross-references, seven centuries before hyperlinks.

But the akhayl carried a dark reputation: it was considered ill-omened (mash'um) — one of those creatures whose very sight made travelers uneasy. And the poet the book quotes made it the very archetype of a bad omen. His verse plays on the word itself: "Leave me to my knowledge of affairs... for my bird-omen over you is no akhayl" — my warning to you isn't some ill-omened thing, it's solid knowledge. The bird's name had become shorthand for "bad sign," the way we might say "that's a bad omen" without thinking about actual birds.

The poem, the name and the grammar debate

Then the grammarians take over, as they always do in this book — and this is where it gets deliciously nerdy. Is akhayl declinable? The ruling: yes in the indefinite (it takes nunation), but NOT when it becomes a proper name. Some grammarians refused to decline it in either case, arguing it was originally an adjective derived from al-takhayyul (imagining, fancying) — and they cited the poet's own verse as evidence, because the poet uses it adjectivally: "bi-akhayla," as an ill-omened thing, not as a bird's name.

A bird entry — four lines about a green bird — and half of it is a grammar debate about whether its name declines. This is the Hayat al-Hayawan method in miniature: the animal is never just the animal. It's a doorway into language, poetry, superstition, and scholarship, all at once.

A green flash of wings, a speckled name, a bad omen, and a grammar debate: seven centuries before modern ornithology, this is how you filed a bird. I kind of love it. The part that stopped me cold is the next one. That snake verse wasn't just floating in the text as decoration. It was recited by a governor at a graveside. The Wild Cow That Split the Dictionary 🐂

Quick Answer

Why could medieval dictionaries not agree on the arkh?

Three authorities defined the arkh differently: a two-year-old unmated female wild cow, any wild cow, or the wild cow’s calf. The disagreement mattered because Arabic poets used the animal as an image of beauty, so the exact age and identity changed what a poetic comparison would mean.

At a glance

  • Three dictionaries give three different definitions.
  • One definition specifies a two-year-old unmated female.
  • A roadside poem uses the arkh as an image of beauty.

Three definitions for one wild cow

In Hayat al-Hayawan, the entire entry for the arkh (الأرخ) is less than half a page, and yet it manages to start a full-blown dictionary fight. The question is simple: what exactly is an arkh?

The grammarian Ibn Durustawayh gives the most precise answer: she is the female two-year-old wild cow that has never been mated by the bull — with not one but two plurals to choose from, urukh and arakh. Read that definition again and marvel at it. Not just a wild cow. A female. Two years old. Never mated. This is a man who spent his life parsing grammar, and he brought the same terrifying precision to zoology. The wild cow, filed by age, sex, and reproductive history.

But the other lexicographers won't agree. And this is where it gets fun. Al-Jawhari, the famous compiler of al-Sihah (the great dictionary), says the arkh is simply the wild cow itself — no age, no condition attached. Strip away Ibn Durustawayh's fine print; an arkh is an arkh. Meanwhile the author of al-Maghrib counters that it is the young of the wild cow — not the adult at all, but the calf. Three dictionaries, three definitions, one animal.

Why the argument mattered to poetry

Now, you might think this is just pedantry, medieval scholars arguing about nothing. But something real is at stake. In a culture where poetry was the supreme art, and the wild cow was one of poetry's supreme images of beauty, the exact meaning of the word mattered enormously. When a poet compared his beloved to an arkh, was he invoking a two-year-old heifer, a full-grown wild cow, or a calf? The image shifts with the definition.

And the entry preserves the poetry to prove it. Ibn Durustawayh himself heard a verse improvised on the road to Mecca by a Bedouin of the Muzayna tribe, who sang of his days with his beloved May: “as if she were an arkh roaming a rich, thriving meadow” (mithqal — the meadow is lush, abundant). Picture the scene: the pilgrimage road, a tribesman singing off the cuff, and the grammarian, on his way to Mecca, stopping to write it down as linguistic evidence. Scholarship with dust on its sandals.

In the desert imagination, the wild cow — graceful, watchful, vanishing into the shimmer — was the highest compliment for beauty. The poets reached for her when they wanted to say: she is lovely, she is wild, she cannot be caught.

So the briefest entry in the book turns out to contain its oldest trick. Half a page, one animal, three warring dictionaries, one roadside song — and a thousand years of Arabic poetry hiding inside. The arkh may be small on the page. In the language, she looms enormous.

This is presented as an old desert superstition, not fact. But it explains why the snake became the perfect symbol for being trapped between two evils. Then the grammarians arrive, because of course they do. Al-Jahiz held that arnab is always feminine; al-Mubarrad disagreed — it applies to both sexes. The male has his own name, al-khuzaz; the female is 'ikrishah; the baby is al-khirniq, growing through stages: khirniq, then sakhla, then arnab. A whole life cycle in three words.

Medieval scholars counted the rabbit among exactly four creatures that menstruate: woman, hyena, bat, and rabbit — though Al-Damiri honestly notes the report's chain is weak. The book keeps showing its working, and I respect that.

Because of that belief, pre-Islamic Arabs held that jinn flee from the rabbit. People hung a rabbit's ankle-bone on themselves as an amulet — the book says they believed no evil eye or magic could touch whoever wore it.

The rabbit also sleeps with its eyes open, Al-Damiri reports, so a hunter might stumble on one mid-nap and mistake it for wide awake. And there was an old claim that if a rabbit sees the sea, it dies — which is why none live on the coasts. Al-Damiri himself writes that this "is not sound in my view": a medieval author fact-checking his own sources, seven centuries before peer review.

The animal courtroom and later traditions

The hunting anecdotes are delightful. Anas ibn Malik reports: "We started a rabbit at Marr al-Zahran; the people chased it and failed, so I caught it and brought it to Abu Talha, who slaughtered it and sent the Prophet its haunch and thigh — and he accepted it." History in a mouthful.

The entry's most delightful story is the animal courtroom. A rabbit found a date, a fox snatched it and ate it, and they took their case to a lizard judge. The dialogue became proverb: the rabbit calls "upon one who hears" (sami'an da'awt); the lizard answers "I have come as a just, wise judge"; and — before hearing a word of evidence — "I have already judged" (qad qadayt). Every flattering reply of the lizard became an Arabic proverb, including "the judge is approached in his own house" — go to power where it sits.

Then there is the sea rabbit — an animal with a rabbit's head and a fish's body. Ibn Sina calls it a small shelled creature so poisonous that drinking from it kills, so eating it is forbidden: the one exception to the rule that the sea version of a land animal is fair game.

The khawass run long, all the book's claims, never advice: rabbit brain roasted with pepper helps trembling; rabbit blood smeared on vitiligo removes it; its rennet with vinegar counters snake venom. And my favorite: rabbit dung fumigated in the bathhouse makes whoever smells it break wind uncontrollably, a fart joke preserved in a medieval encyclopedia for 700 years. Some things are timeless.

A rabbit in your dream means a beautiful woman — who is not friendly. If slaughtered, a wife who will not remain. Eating cooked rabbit meat: provision from an unexpected source.

The Mountain Goat That Nursed a Prophet 🏔️

Quick Answer

How did the arwiya help the Prophet Jonah?

In Hayat al-Hayawan, GOD sends a female ibex, the arwiya, to nurse Jonah after he is cast ashore. She crouches over him and gives him milk morning and evening until his flesh returns. The mountain animal later becomes a symbol of protection, rarity, endurance, and devoted motherhood.

At a glance

  • The arwiya nurses Jonah in the book’s account.
  • Its mountain sure-footedness becomes a metaphor for protection.
  • A human lawsuit helped create a false belief that ibexes were blind.

A mountain goat in sacred stories

The arwiya (الأروية) is the female ibex — the wild mountain goat of the high peaks — and her entry in Al-Damiri's book opens straight into the story of the Prophet Jonah. When Jonah (Yunus) was cast ashore, the book says, GOD caused a gourd to grow over him and sent a wild arwiya that would crouch over him and suckle him with her milk morning and evening until his flesh grew back. A mountain goat, pressed into service as a prophet's nurse.

And here's a detail that stopped me: Ibn al-Jawzi, citing al-Hasan on the verse "and We ransomed him with a great sacrifice," reports that the sacrifice came from the arwiya — wild ibexes sent down to Jonah from Mount Thabir. A striking alternative to the familiar ram of the sacrifice story, with mountain goats descending from the peaks instead.

The ibex earned her place in the Prophet's own life, too. The book records that he was once gifted an arwiya while in the pilgrim state (ihram). And at Uhud, Abdullah ibn Umar described his own desperate climb up the mountain: "I was climbing like the arwiya climbs" — clambering hand over hand until he reached the Prophet. The ibex as the very image of the unscalable made scalable.

Proverbs, protection and mistaken identity

That image became theology. Al-Tirmidhi records: "Religion will cling to Medina as the snake clings to its hole, and religion will become protected in the Hijaz as the arwiya is protected on the mountaintop." The ibex on her unreachable peak — the perfect metaphor for an untouchable stronghold.

The ibex family also supplied one of Arabic's most cutting proverbs. A man named 'Awf once heard someone slip so clumsily in speech that he said the fellow had "combined the arwa with the ostrich" — the ibex lives on the mountain tops, the ostrich on the open plains; two contradictories joined. Say it when two things could not be more opposite. And because the shy ibex is glimpsed by people perhaps once in a lifetime, "like the coming and going of the arwa" became the proverb for rare acts of kindness.

But the entry's strangest legacy is a case of mistaken identity that lasted centuries. A woman named Arwa bint Uways falsely accused Sa'id bin Zayd — one of the ten promised Paradise — of stealing her land. He surrendered the land and prayed: "O GOD, if she is lying, blind her sight and make her grave in her well." She went blind, a flood exposed the true boundaries, vindicating him, and she fell into her own well and died. The people of Medina thereafter cursed their enemies with "may GOD blind him as He blinded Arwa" — and generations later, listeners heard al-arwa, the mountain goat, and concluded the IBEX was proverbially blind. A false lawsuit became a false zoological fact.

The book credits the ibex with one more marvel: a tenderness toward her young so fierce that if one is caught in a snare, she follows it and stays in the snare with it — plus the claim that it breathes through two holes in its horns, and dies quickly if they are blocked. And the khawass: mix the ibex's horn and hoof in oil, rub it on a weary traveler's body and legs, and it removes the harm of fatigue "as if he had not walked at all." The book's claim, not ours — history, never advice.

The Red Worm That Turns Into a Butterfly 🦋

Quick Answer

What is the red worm that becomes a butterfly?

The asari’ are described as red caterpillars that live in herbs or sand and shed their skins to become butterflies. Their entry became a linguistic detective story because Al-Damiri found that a copyist may have changed “sand” to “herbs,” creating centuries of disagreement from one handwritten mistake.

At a glance

  • The caterpillars are said to molt into butterflies.
  • Sources disagree over whether they live in sand or herbs.
  • Al-Damiri traces the disagreement to a possible scribal error.

A caterpillar with disputed habitat

The asari' (الأساريع) — red caterpillars that live in herbs and shed their skins to become butterflies — earned a full entry in Al-Damiri's book, mostly because medieval scholars could not agree on what exactly they were. And the disagreement starts with the very pronunciation: the hamza takes a fatha — it's a-SARI', not i-SARI' — and the singular is asroo'.

Some described them as red-headed, white-bodied worms in the sand, "like the fingers of women" — a description recorded in al-Kifaya and confirmed by Ibn Qutayba in Adab al-Katib. Others placed them in the herbs and called them banat al-naqa, "the daughters of the she-camel." Ibn al-Sikkit insisted they live in the sand and molt into butterflies, while Ibn Malik devoted a whole linguistic treatise to whether the word should be asari' or yasari' — this is a book in which the grammar of worms received serious scholarship.

Al-Damiri catches what looks like a 1,000-year-old typo — my favorite find in this entry. Ibn Malik's book attributes to Ibn al-Sikkit the claim that the worm lives in herbs (baql) and molts into a butterfly. But al-Damiri checked Ibn al-Sikkit's actual work, Islah al-Mantiq — and there, Ibn al-Sikkit says they live in the sand (raml). Al-Damiri's conclusion: some copyist, somewhere in the chain of manuscripts, miscopied raml as baql. One slip of the pen, and centuries of scholars argued about whether the worm lives in sand or herbs — when the whole debate may rest on a scribe's bad handwriting. Medieval textual criticism, solving a cold case.

The thousand-year-old copying mystery

The book also corrects one popular mix-up: some people claimed the asari' were shahmat al-ard, "the fat of the earth" — but no, that is a different creature entirely, promised for a later chapter.

Eating them is forbidden (they count as insects), but the khawass — the "special properties" chapter where medieval medicine lives — credits the crushed worm with a startling power: smeared on a cut nerve or tendon, it heals it immediately, "with great benefit." That is the book's claim, not ours: a 700-year-old folk remedy, presented here as history, never as advice.

The dream interpreters had their own take. If the yasroo' shows up in your dreams, it means a thief who steals a little at a time, dresses himself in piety, and fools nobody — because, the book notes, this green worm of the cucumber vines and vineyards cannot hide its hypocrisy. The dream ends on the thief, exposed: a small red worm carrying a whole medieval worldview.

The Falcon That Named a Color 🦅

Quick Answer

How did a falcon’s coloring become an Arabic color word?

Hayat al-Hayawan calls the falcon al-asfa’ because all falcons carry saf’, a darkness mixed with red. The same color word describes a woman’s dark cheeks and the dark patch on a pigeon’s neck, so a three-line bird entry becomes a compact map of how one visual description traveled through language.

At a glance

  • The original entry is only three lines long.
  • Saf’ means darkness mixed with redness.
  • The word links a falcon, a woman’s cheeks and a pigeon’s neck.

A falcon named for its coloring

The entire entry for the falcon — al-asfa', الأسفع — is three lines long. Three lines. And those three lines are barely about the bird at all — they're about a word that refused to stay a name.

Al-Damiri opens with the definition: al-asfa' is the falcon (الصقر), “and all falcons are saf'.” That last clause is doing all the work. Saf' isn't a species — it's a look. A darkness touched with red, like embers cooling under ash. And al-Damiri is telling you every falcon wears it. The bird didn't get a name so much as a description that stuck.

Because saf'a — السفعة, with a damma on the seen — means exactly that: darkness mixed with redness. In the face, the dictionaries say, it is a darkness on a woman's cheeks. To prove the word, al-Damiri reaches past ornithology entirely, into the Sahih — the hadith collections — where a woman is described as saf'a al-khaddayn: dark-cheeked. A raptor's dusky cheek patch becomes the standard word for a woman's complexion. The falcon's face, fossilized in a hadith about a woman's face.

One word across bird and human faces

Then comes the final line, and honestly it's my favorite of the three: the pigeon is called saf'a too — for the dark coloration on its neck. Same word, third animal. One falcon, one woman, one pigeon, all sharing a single Arabic root that means “dark, with a hint of red.”

I love what this reveals about how the medieval mind organized nature. A modern field guide would sort the falcon by talons, wingspan, and hunting behavior. Al-Damiri sorts it by color vocabulary. The bird is filed under a word, and the word is defined by everything else it touches — cheeks, necks, embers. The bestiary is half natural history, half dictionary, and al-Damiri walks between the two like they were always the same room.

It also shows how close the animal kingdom was to everyday life. You didn't need a falcon in front of you to know saf' — you needed a cheek, or a pigeon on the windowsill. The book assumes its reader has seen all three. That's a whole world in one assumption: falcons overhead, women in the gathering, pigeons everywhere, and one word doing duty for all of them.

Three lines. A bird, a woman, a pigeon, and a color. Al-Damiri never tells us a single story about the falcon's hunting or its habits — he gives us something stranger instead: a name that outlived the bird it was meant for, and kept on describing the world after the bird had flown past.

The Snake That Turned Up in Three Graves 🐍

Quick Answer

Why does the black shedding snake appear in three graves?

In one story, mourners find the same black shedding snake in three graves dug for a dishonest food seller. Ibn Abbas tells them the snake represents the man’s deeds and would appear wherever they buried him. The entry frames the repeated serpent as divine punishment for commercial fraud.

At a glance

  • The snake is intensely black and sheds its skin yearly.
  • The same serpent appears in three burial niches.
  • Two charity stories show bread repelling a deadly snake.

The feared black snake in language and prayer

Some animals get an entry about their habits. The aswad salikh — the black shedding snake — gets an entry about the dead.

Al-Damiri starts with the natural history: the aswad salikh is a kind of uf'uwan, a giant serpent, intensely black, named for shedding its skin every year. Then he does what he always does — he hands the microphone to the grammarians. You say aswad salikh, never salikha for the female, on the authority of al-Asma'i and Abu Zayd; Ibn Zayd reported the feminine form, but the first is more famous. The plurals: asawid salikha, sawalik. A snake entry that doubles as a grammar lesson. Only in this book.

Then the hadiths, and they are not gentle. Abu Dawud, al-Nasa'i, and al-Hakim (who graded it authentic) report from Abdullah ibn Umar that the Prophet, when night fell on a journey, would say: “O earth — my Lord and your Lord is GOD. I seek refuge in GOD from your evil, from the evil of what is in you, from the evil of what was created in you, and from the evil of what crawls upon you. I seek refuge in GOD from the lion and the black one, from the snake and the scorpion, from the dweller of the town, and from the father and what was born.” The book glosses the last line: the town-dweller is the jinn; the father and what was born, Iblis and the devils. The black snake is named in the same breath as lions and devils — that tells you exactly how the desert felt about it.

Bukhari and Muslim add the harder edge: the Prophet commanded that the “two black ones” — the snake and the scorpion — be killed even during prayer. Nothing, not even worship, outranks them as a threat.

The poets got their turns too. Ibn Hisham quotes verses in Kitab al-Tijan where a sleepless eye is “kohled with the poison of the black one.” And Imam al-Shafi'i, in a line I can't get over, calls the eloquent poet himself a shedding black snake — “and poetry is his saliva and his spittle.” Imagine the greatest jurist of his age telling you that a smooth-talking poet is, zoologically speaking, a venomous reptile. The enmity of poets, he adds, is an incurable disease.

Three graves, two loaves and the moral turn

Then the stories.

A group of pilgrims loses a companion at a place called al-Sifah. They dig his grave. In the burial niche: a black shedding snake, stretched across the whole of it. They dig a second grave. Same snake, same position. A third. Same. They give up and go to Ibn Abbas, who tells them: “That is his deed, the one he used to do. Go and bury him in one of them — by GOD, if you dug up the whole earth, you would find the same.” Back home, the dead man's wife explains everything: he sold food, kept his family's daily share, then mixed in an equal measure of barley straw and sold the lot. A cheat, in other words — and his grave kept sprouting the same black snake. The book presents it as divine punishment for fraud; I present it as one of the eeriest images in medieval literature.

A gentler miracle follows. The Prophet, needing privacy, sits under a tree and takes off his sandals, slipping one back on — when a bird swoops down, snatches the other, and carries it skyward. Out of the sandal tumbles a black shedding snake. “This is an honor by which GOD honored me,” he says: “O GOD, I seek refuge in You from the evil of what walks on its belly, and from the evil of what walks on two legs, and from the evil of what walks on four.” Al-Damiri notes a sister-hadith lives under the crow's entry.

And then the entry's great pair: two stories, centuries apart, with the same skeleton. A man of the prophet Saleh's people — a troublemaker, the kind the community begged Saleh to pray against — goes out with two loaves of bread, eats one, gives one to a beggar. He comes home safe from the firewood run. Saleh tells him to untie his bundle: inside, a black shedding snake “like a tree trunk,” jaws clamped on a thick log. “By this it was repelled from you,” Saleh says — the charity. Then the second: travelers pass Jesus, son of Mary; he says one of them will die that day. Evening comes, they return with their firewood; he has the marked man untie his bundle — a black snake inside. What did the man do that day? Given part of a piece of bread to a poor man. “By it, it was repelled from you.”

Two prophets, two snakes, two loaves. The book's moral architecture is not subtle: charity buys you out of a death sentence, and the snake is the sentence.

From grammar to graves to two loaves of bread — all of it under a snake named for shedding its skin. The aswad salikh sloughs off its old body every year, and this entry sloughs off any single genre: dictionary, hadith collection, poetry anthology, ghost story, sermon.

The Man Who Entered Paradise Without Praying a Single Prayer

Quick Answer

Why are the wolf and crow called al-asraman?

The wolf and crow are called al-asraman, “the cut-off ones,” because both keep their distance from people. The same dual word can also name night and day, which remain separate. Al-Damiri then links the term with Usayrim, a man who accepted Islam at Uhud and entered Paradise without a recorded prayer.

At a glance

  • One dual Arabic word names both wolf and crow.
  • The root means to break away or separate.
  • The entry closes with the story of Usayrim at Uhud.

The wolf, the crow and a shared name

Some entries in Al-Damiri's book are about one animal. This one is about two animals sharing a single name — and then, with no warning at all, it tells you the most astonishing story about a man I have come across in this entire book.

The word is al-asraman — الأصرمان. It is a dual form, which means it names a pair. And the pair is: the wolf and the crow. One word, two animals. That's the first strange fact right there — in classical Arabic, the wolf and the crow are a matched set, like salt and pepper.

Why those two? The great grammarian Ibn al-Sikkit gives the answer: because both of them cut themselves off from people. The Arabic root behind the name is insarama — to break away, to separate. Wolves live far from human settlements, and crows — the wild ones of the desert — keep their distance too. Two creatures united by the single trait of refusing our company. Seven hundred years ago, somebody looked at that behavior and gave them a shared name: “the cut-off ones.”

Here is the part I love most, though. The same word, al-asraman, is also used for something else entirely: night and day. Because night is cut off from day, and day is cut off from night — neither one bleeds into the other, they just hand the world over, back and forth, forever. The linguists who built this language had a poet's eye. Two animals that avoid humans; two halves of time that never meet. One word for both.

Usayrim’s single day as a believer

And then the entry does what this book always does — it drops the dictionary and hands you a story. Imam Ahmad reports, with a sound chain from Abu Hurayra, that he used to ask people a riddle: “Tell me of a man who entered Paradise but never prayed a single prayer.” People would puzzle over it. Then he would give the answer: Usayrim ibn Abd al-Ashhal.

Usayrim had spent years refusing Islam while his whole people accepted it. He held out, stubborn, one of the last holdouts. Then came the day of Uhud. The Prophet, peace be upon him, marched out with the army — and something broke open in Usayrim. Islam “dawned on him,” the book says, that very morning. He took his sword, walked straight into the battle, fought beside the believers until he was killed.

When they told the Prophet about him afterward, he said: “He is among the people of Paradise.”

A man who refused the message for years, accepted it on the morning of a battle, died that same afternoon — and was promised Paradise before the sun set on his first day as a believer. No recorded prayer. No fasting. No pilgrimage. Just one sincere turn of the heart, and a sword raised for it.

Al-Damiri tucked this story under an entry about wolves and crows. The asraman are the ones who stay apart — and Usayrim was the one who stayed apart from everyone, until the day he didn't. He cut himself off, and then he cut himself back in.

The One-Legged Snake That Kills With a Look

Quick Answer

Why is the aslah the book’s most terrifying snake?

The aslah combines nearly every frightening trait in Hayat al-Hayawan: a huge head, a short body that leaps at riders, one leg in one account, a human-like face after a thousand years, and a stare said to kill. Other passages say its touch burns and its name suggests extermination.

At a glance

  • The snake is said to leap onto mounted riders.
  • One account gives it a single leg and a spinning attack.
  • Its stare and touch are described as deadly medieval properties.

The leaping, one-legged desert terror

Every snake entry in Al-Damiri's book is frightening in its own way. The black shedding snake gets three graves and two prophets. But the aslah — الأصلة — gets something stranger: according to the book, it might have one leg, it might have a human face, and it can kill you just by looking at you.

The grammarian Ibn al-Anbari records the basics: the aslah is a snake with a huge head and a short body, and it does not slither up to you — it leaps onto the horseman and kills him. A short-bodied snake that jumps onto a mounted rider. The desert Arabs who named it were not people who exaggerated lightly; this thing terrified them.

Then comes the detail that stopped me cold. Another account in the entry says the aslah is a malignant snake with one leg. It stands upright on that single leg, spins around in a circle, and then springs. Its plural, tellingly, is usul — a whole category of them. I have no idea what animal this describes. A one-legged snake that does a war dance before it strikes sounds like a nightmare the desert invented — but the book records it in the same calm tone it uses for grammar.

The poet al-Asma'i quotes a verse that shows how deeply this creature lived in the Arab imagination. It's a curse: “O Lord, if Yazid has eaten the flesh of his friend, bite after bite — then decree for him an aslah from the usals, hollow as a round loaf or a camel's hoof.” Imagine hating someone so much that your prayer is not for lightning or defeat, but for this specific snake to find him. The aslah was the worst thing the poet's mind could reach for.

Fire, a human face and death by sight

Al-Jahiz, the great naturalist, adds the Bedouin claim: nothing passes by the aslah without catching fire. Whatever it touches burns. And the name itself, he says, comes from isti'sal — uprooting, extermination. It is named for its power to destroy. A snake so venomous the book describes its effect as burning.

It even shows up in eschatology. A hadith describing the Dajjal — the false messiah of Islamic end-times tradition — says “his head is like an aslah.” Think about what that tells you: when a first-century audience needed an image for the most terrifying face imaginable, they reached for this snake. Its head was the ceiling of horror.

Then the entry's oldest and wildest claim: some said the aslah's face is like a human face — huge, massive — and that it becomes this way after living a thousand years. A millennium-old snake with a man's face, burning everything it passes, leaping onto riders. And its final listed property, among the khawass — the occult properties the medieval writers catalogued — is the simplest and most chilling of all: it kills by looking at it. The book claims the aslah can strike you dead with a stare.

Grammar lesson, curse-poem, apocalyptic hadith, a thousand-year-old human face, and death by eye contact — all under one snake's name. The aslah may be the most overqualified monster in the entire book.

Quick Answer

How did a dusty-black wolf become an image of good government?

The atlas (الأطلس) is the dusty-black wolf of medieval Arabic literature — named not for a species but for a color, “the wolf whose color has dustiness in it, toward black.” Al-Damiri’s entry barely describes the animal; instead it preserves an Umayyad praise poem in which the wolf at a watering trough becomes an image of perfect rule — a poem so famous that the lexicographer al-Jawhari cited it as proof for a point of Arabic grammar.

At a glance

  • Atlas names a dusty color tending toward black.
  • A wolf and camel share a safe watering place in the poem.

A wolf named for a dusty-black shade

The first surprise in al-Damiri's entry for the atlas — الأطلس — is that it isn't a species. It's a color. “The wolf whose color has dustiness in it, toward black,” the book says, “and everything of that color is atlas.” Read that twice. The wolf is filed under a shade the way you'd file paint swatches. An animal named for its grayness the way we call cars “silver.”

The whole entry is only a few lines long, and almost none of them are about wolves. Instead we get a poem — a real one, by the Umayyad poet al-Kumayt, written to praise a man named Muhammad ibn Sulayman al-Hashimi. And the wolf is the entire point of the compliment.

“At Muhammad's watering troughs,” al-Kumayt says, “you find safety — a mangy she-camel and a dusty-black wolf.” Think about what that image meant to people who actually lived among wolves. A watering trough in the desert is where everything thirsty has to come eventually: the prey, the herder's camel, and the predator. In any ordinary world, a wolf at the trough is a catastrophe. In Muhammad's world, the poet is saying, the wolf stands there drinking and nobody flinches. That was his measure of the man's protection — that even the natural order holds its breath.

The next line turns the image into political theory: “Neither this one is feared, nor does that one have boldness — the subjects are guided as long as the leader stays straight.” The wolf and the camel are the whole kingdom in miniature: predator and prey, strong and weak, all behaving because the ruler is behaving. I've read political science that said less, in more words.

And then — this is the part that got me — the wolf does one more job. The great lexicographer al-Jawhari quoted this exact poem as evidence for a grammar point: the verse uses the word “rayyis,” and he cited it to prove that ra'is (leader, chief) can be pronounced “rayyis,” the way qawim becomes qayyim. A wolf poem, filed as a dictionary citation. Al-Damiri includes the whole thing without blinking, because in his world a wolf entry, a praise poem, and a pronunciation rule all belonged in the same paragraph.

That's what keeps pulling me back to this entry. We keep nature in one book, poetry in another, and grammar in a third. Al-Damiri keeps them in one entry. The dusty-black wolf walks in as an animal, leaves as a metaphor for just rule, and on its way out settles a debate about how to say the word “leader.” A few lines, and the wolf never once stops working.

The Animal That Was Four Animals at Once 🐢

Quick Answer

Why does the word utum refer to four different animals?

Medieval authorities used utum for a sea turtle, a thick-skinned fish, a hedgehog, and a cow. Hayat al-Hayawan does not choose among them; it records how one word moved across sea, desert, and farmyard through resemblance, especially the tough hide shared by the fish and the cow.

At a glance

  • The proposed animals are turtle, fish, hedgehog and cow.
  • The fish’s hide was said to be made into camel drivers’ boots.
  • The cow’s name may come from its resemblance to the fish’s hide.

Four animals competing for one name

Every so often, al-Damiri's book gives up on identifying an animal entirely — and the entry for the utum — الأطوم — is the best example I've found. The entry is one paragraph long. It contains four completely different animals. And the book never picks a winner.

The great lexicographer al-Jawhari opens the bidding: he says the utum is like the anuq — the sea turtle. Fine. A sea turtle; we can picture that. But then other scholars weigh in with something else: no, it's a fish. A fish with skin so thick and tough it's like a camel's hide — and here is the detail that makes this entry worth the price of admission — they made boots out of it. Boots, for camel drivers. The medieval world was stranger than we usually give it credit for.

Then the entry swerves again. Some said the utum is the hedgehog. And some — this one is Ibn Sidah, a serious scholar — said it's the cow. The cow, he explains, was named utum by resemblance to the fish: its hide is so thick it recalls the fish's hide. So the cow is named after the fish, which may not be the fish, which may be the turtle. A chain of resemblances, each link pointing at a different animal.

Fish-hide boots and a wandering word

What I love about this entry is what it shows about how medieval natural history actually worked. Nobody panics. Nobody throws out the disagreement or demands a recount. Al-Damiri just lines up the opinions — turtle, fish, hedgehog, cow — and lets them stand next to each other like specimens on a shelf. The method isn't “find the single correct truth.” It's “record everything the word ever touched.” The utum isn't really an animal so much as a word that wandered through the sea, the desert, and the farmyard, collecting meanings the way a fishing net collects whatever it drags through.

I think it's something more honest. When you don't know what a word's animal is, you write down everything you heard — and centuries later, the confusion itself becomes the document. We learn less about the turtle, and more about the people who named things.

And the boots stay with me. Somewhere in the 1300s, a camel driver may have walked the caravan routes in fish-hide boots, and al-Damiri thought that was important enough to preserve in a book of wonders. He was right. Of all the animals in the entry, that's the one I can see.

    The article lists four competing identifications. Al-Jawhari compares the utum with the anuq, or sea turtle. Other scholars call it a thick-skinned fish, some identify it as a hedgehog, and Ibn Sidah says it can mean a cow. Al-Damiri records every view without choosing a winner.

  2. What were utum fish skins used for?

    One definition describes a fish with skin as thick and tough as a camel’s hide. The article says that this skin was made into boots worn by camel drivers. That detail links a sea animal with desert travel and gives the brief dictionary entry one of its most vivid surviving images.

  3. Why could utum also mean a cow?

    Ibn Sidah explains that the cow was called utum because its thick hide resembled the hide of the fish bearing the same name. The article presents this as a chain of resemblance: the cow points to the fish, while other authorities point the word toward a turtle or hedgehog instead.

Quick Answer

I opened the entry on the ataysh expecting a bird. What I got was one line about a bird — and then two full pages about one of the most brilliant minds in Islamic history. This is my favorite kind of accident in this book.

Here is the whole bird: the ataysh is a bird, on the authority of Ibn Sidah — and the word taysh means lightness of mind. That's it. No habitat, no habits, no hunting stories. The great lexicographer Ibn Sidah named it, and al-Damiri filed it under a word about being light-headed. You can already feel where this is going.

Because taysh is the exact word Imam al-Shafi'i once used about the most learned man in Egypt: “I never saw anyone more knowledgeable in the law than Ashhab — were it not for the taysh in him.” One sentence, and the whole entry derails. A bird called “the flighty one” reminds al-Damiri of the most famous use of the word, and suddenly we're not in ornithology anymore. We're in a biography.

Ashhab — Ashhab ibn Abd al-Aziz, the Maliki jurist of Egypt — was born in the same year as al-Shafi'i, 150 AH, and died eighteen days after him. Ibn Abd al-Hakam reports that he heard Ashhab openly praying for al-Shafi'i's death. Al-Shafi'i answered in verse — men wish for my death, he said, and if I die, that is a road on which I am not the first traveler; tell the one who wants otherwise to prepare for the same. Then, the book says simply: al-Shafi'i died. And Ashhab — the man who had prayed for it — bought a slave from al-Shafi'i's estate. The narrator adds, with a detail you can't invent, that he himself bought that same slave from Ashhab's estate thirty days later. Death, rivalry, and an estate sale, all in one paragraph.

But the entry keeps going — into the famous dream. When al-Shafi'i's mother was carrying him, the book reports, she dreamed that Jupiter burst apart above Egypt and a fragment fell into every town. The interpreters told her a scholar would come from her, one whose knowledge would spread across Egypt and beyond. Prophecy or legend, it's a hell of an origin story.

Then the small human details that make a twelve-century-old scholar feel alive. Fresh dates were brought to him once, and he talked to them like an old friend: how sweet and delicious you are — but knowledge is sweeter and more delicious than you. He wouldn't eat them. He bought a slave-girl and spent the whole night studying while she waited; she told the broker he'd locked her up with a madman. His reply is still quoted today: the madman is the one who knows the worth of knowledge and wastes it.

The book closes with the scholars' unanimous verdict — his trustworthiness, piety, devotion — notes he was the first to write on jurisprudence's principles, and records the dates: born in Gaza in 150 AH, the year Abu Hanifa died; carried to Mecca at six; reached Egypt in 199; died in 204 at fifty-four. His tomb in Cairo's Qarafa cemetery is famous to this day.

So: the ataysh. A bird. One line. And hiding underneath it, one of the great biographical digressions in medieval literature — all because a bird's name was the word a genius used for his rival's one flaw. I've read longer chapters that said less.

Goose Facts: The Feather on the Thief's Head and the Cry That Warned a Caliph 🪿

Quick Answer

In Hayat al-Hayawan, the goose (الإوز) is a water bird whose gosling swims the moment it hatches and whose mate stands guard over the nest without blinking. The entry preserves two famous stories: Solomon catching a goose thief by the feather on his head, and geese screaming at Ali ibn Abi Talib on the morning of his assassination.

If you only know geese as the angry birds of the park pond, this entry will change your mind. The goose facts in al-Damiri's book come wrapped in poetry, a prophet's detective story, and a dawn chorus that turned out to be a funeral announcement.

Goose Facts From the First Line: How to Say “Goose” in 1372

Al-Damiri opens with the word itself. Al-iwazz (الإوز) — hamza with a kasra, waw with a fatha, in case you were wondering how a 14th-century scholar wanted you to pronounce “goose.” One goose is an iwazza (إوزة); a flock is awzun (أوزون). I love that the book treats pronunciation as natural history — before it tells you a single thing the bird does, it makes sure you can say its name.

The Poet Who Heard Pens in Their Honking

Then the microphone goes to Abu Nuwas — the great Abbasid poet of wine and wit, al-Hasan ibn Hani (d. 194 AH). He heard geese honking as pens scratching across parchment: a flock rising off the water, whistling as if from spoons, the squeak of quills on writing sheets. The Gosling That Swims at Birth

Then the real goose facts, and they're lovely. Geese love swimming. The chick leaves the egg and swims immediately — no lessons, no practice. While the female sits on the eggs, the male stands guard and never leaves her side for the blink of an eye. The chicks hatch in the last days of the month.

The Feather on the Thief's Head

Now the story this entry should be famous for. A man complained to Solomon, peace be upon him, that his neighbors were stealing his geese. Solomon called out, “Prayer is assembling!” When the people gathered, he preached — and said, almost in passing: “One of you steals his neighbor's geese, then walks into the mosque with a feather on his head.” Somewhere in the crowd, a man reached up and wiped his head. Solomon pointed: “Seize him — he's your man.” The book cites it from al-Dinawari and Ibn al-Jawzi, on the authority of Muhammad ibn Ka'b al-Qurazi. Seven centuries later, “the feather gave him away” is still detection in a single gesture — and GOD had given Solomon a wisdom no lock could keep out.

“Leave Them — They Are Mourners”

The entry's other great scene is darker. Imam Ahmad reports — through Husayn ibn Kathir, from his father, who had met Ali himself — that Ali ibn Abi Talib walked out to the dawn prayer as geese screamed in his face. The people shooed them away. Ali stopped them: “Leave them, for they are mourners.” That same morning, Ibn Muljam struck him down. Ali heard a funeral in the honking of geese — and he was right.

What the Old Doctors Believed About Goose Meat

Every entry ends with khawass — the properties — and the standard warning applies: this is what the old books claimed, not advice. Hippocrates called the goose the moistest of all domestic birds. Its meat is very hot and moist: it fattens the body but fills it with waste, so the book advises borax blown into the throat before slaughter, oil rubbed on the meat before roasting, and plenty of hot spices. Hard to digest, quick to bring on fevers. The fat was believed to help pleurisy and hair loss; the tongue, urinary dripping; a stone from the goose's stomach, drunk in water, looseness of the bowels. The eggs: best half-boiled, good for hot constitutions, bad for colic — take them with thyme and salt, and eat the yolk, not the white.

Ilq is the wolf. The female is al-qa (القة). The plural of both is al-qaw (الق). Three forms, one line, no wasted ink. Arabic does this the way old English did with deer and hart and hind — the animal changes its name with its sex and number, and you were expected to know all three.

Why? The book doesn't say. Maybe the word-collectors just wrote down what they heard desert people saying — rules, exceptions, and all. Either way, it's the strangest half-sentence on the page, and it's why this entry will stay in my memory long after the longer ones fade.

The book's wolf vocabulary is scattered across the alphabet like footprints — and we haven't even reached the main entry yet.

Al-Aws: The Wolf That Fed Another Animal's Baby — and the Saint Named After It 🐺

Quick Answer

Al-aws (الأوس) is the Arabic word for “the wolf” — and also a man's name, because Arabs literally named their sons after wolves. This Hayat al-Hayawan entry runs from a wolf feeding a hunted hyena's cub, to a wolf at a funeral the Prophet called “Uwais,” to the great saint Uwais al-Qarni, who carried the same word.

Al-Aws Means Wolf. Parents Named Their Kids “Wolf.”

The entry opens with plain etymology: al-aws means “the wolf” (الذئب), “and by it the man was named.” Arabs really did name their sons after the wolf — the way other cultures use lion or eagle names.

Then it gets tender. “Uwais” (أويس) is the diminutive of aws — a little wolf, so to speak — built on the same pattern as Kumayt and Lujayn. Diminutives in Arabic are often affectionate. The desert's most feared sheep-raider had a pet-name form.

The Wolf That Raised Another Animal's Baby

The poets knew the wolf's day job: livestock theft. Al-Hudhali wrote, “I wish I knew about you — and the matter is momentous — what Uwais did to the sheep today.” The wolf, named like a person, addressed like gossip.

But al-Kumayt's verse takes a turn I didn't expect: “Just as Umm Amir dozed in her embrace... until Aws raised her children.” Al-Jawhari explains: when a hyena (nicknamed “Umm Amir”) is hunted down, and she has a cub fathered by a wolf, the wolf keeps feeding her cub until it grows up. The wolf whose mate was killed doesn't abandon the orphan — he raises it. The book promises the fuller version under al-isbar, the hyena-wolf hybrid entry, later in book order.

The Wolf at the Funeral

Al-Hafiz Abu Nu'im reports, through Hamza ibn Asad al-Harithi: the Prophet went out to Baqi' al-Gharqad for the funeral of an Ansari man — and there was a wolf lying there, “spread out on its forearms.” The Prophet said: “This is Uwais — so make a stipend for him.” And they did not.

A wolf showed up at a funeral, got called by a human name, and was ordered a stipend. Nobody paid it. The book promises more later: the delegation of wolves, filed under al-dhi'b in the dhal chapter.

The Saint Who Carried the Wolf's Name

And this is where the word becomes a person: “By this name was named Uwais ibn Amir al-Qarni — he reached the Prophet's time but did not see him, and he lived in Kufa, and he is among the greatest of the Tabi'in.”

A man really was named after the wolf-word, and he became one of the most beloved figures of early Islamic history. Muslim reports, through Umar: the Prophet said, “The best of the Tabi'in is a man called Uwais al-Qarni. He will come to you with the reinforcements of the people of Yemen. If he swears by GOD, GOD fulfills it — so if you can have him seek forgiveness for you, do so.” When Uwais came to Umar, Umar asked, and he sought forgiveness for him. Uwais was later killed at Siffin alongside Ali.

Then the entry closes with reports about his rank: Ahmad ibn Hanbal in al-Zuhd, from al-Hasan al-Basri — “More will enter Paradise by the intercession of a man from my ummah than the tribes of Rabi'a and Mudar.” Al-Hasan said: it is Uwais al-Qarni — adding that al-Jawhari made a famous error about the Qarn attribution. A word that starts as “the wolf” ends up belonging to a man the Prophet called the best of a generation.

The Serpent That Performed the Hajj: Seven Circles Around the Ka'ba 🐍

Quick Answer

Al-aym (الأيم) is the male serpent in Hayat al-Hayawan — and its entry holds the strangest pilgrim story in the whole book. Al-Azraqi's History of Mecca reports a gleaming serpent emerging from the Banu Shayba gate, circling the Ka'ba seven times, praying two rak'as behind the Maqam, and then rising toward the sky and vanishing before witnesses. That, more or less, is the entire entry: a snake that did Hajj.

The Name: Al-Aym, the Male Serpent

Short entry, so let me get the words right. Al-aym and al-ayn are the book's word for the serpent — the big snake. Al-Azraqi, in his History of Mecca, specifies that al-aym means the male of the serpent. The grammarian Ibn al-Sikkit adds a language note: the word was originally “aym” and got lightened the way Arabic softens similar words — like lin from layn — with the plural ayyum. Dry linguistics, but it tells you something: this is a real word from the old Meccan vocabulary, not a fairy-tale invention.

Then comes the story. And the story is the whole reason this entry exists.

Seven Circles, Two Rak'as

Talq ibn Habib narrates it — and it's one of those accounts the book gives you straight, with a chain of transmission and named witnesses, like a hadith. “We were sitting with Abdullah ibn Amr ibn al-As in the Hijr” — the walled enclosure beside the Ka'ba — “when the shade shrank and the gathering stood.” Midday. Everyone dispersing.

“And then we saw a gleaming aym coming out of the gate of Banu Shayba.”

A gleaming serpent, emerging from the gate like it had business there. “And the eyes of the people rose to it.” Of course they did.

Then — and this is the part that makes the entry unforgettable — the serpent circled the House seven times and prayed two rak'as behind the Maqam. Full tawaf. Full prayer. A snake performing the rites better than most of the humans watching.

The people went to it and addressed it directly — “O pilgrim” (mu'tamir), they said, “GOD has completed your rites. But in our land there are slaves and fools, and we fear for you from them.” They weren't afraid of the serpent. They were afraid the local idiots would hurt the holy serpent.

“Then it departed toward the sky, and we did not see it.”

And Yet: Kill the Aym

And then the entry turns on a dime. From the hadith: he commanded that the aym be killed.

That is medieval natural history for you: it records the world as it was reported, contradictions included, and leaves the reader sitting with them.

Al-Ayl: The Stag That Hunts Venomous Snakes and Cries Poison-Curing Stones 🦌

Quick Answer

Al-ayl (الأيل) is the male ibex in Hayat al-Hayawan — a mountain stag that hurls itself off cliffs unharmed, hunts venomous snakes for dinner, befriends fish at the seashore, and weeps tears that harden into stones the book claims cure poison. Its antlers, shed every year, double as medicine, fish-lures, and — per the entry — an alarm clock you can never sleep through.

The Mountain Stag With a Death Wish

Let's start with the headline stunt. The al-ayl — the male of the ibexes, called kuzan in Persian, and in most of its habits resembling wild cattle — has a survival strategy no zoologist would recommend: when it fears the hunter, it throws itself off the top of the mountain. And it is not harmed by it.

Not “probably unharmed.” The entry states it flatly: it hurls itself from the mountain peak and suffers nothing. Seven hundred years before extreme sports, al-Damiri's ibex was base-jumping without a chute.

Its age, meanwhile, is written on its head — the number of its years equals the number of knots on its horns. A living tree-ring system, carved in keratin.

The Snake-Eater

Here is where the al-ayl gets weird. This gentle-looking mountain animal is, according to the book, obsessed with eating snakes. It seeks them out wherever it finds them, and when it eats one, it starts at the tail and works its way to the head — like a connoisseur eating a very dangerous banana.

Naturally, this backfires sometimes. When a snake bites back, the al-ayl's medicine is... crab. If a serpent stings it, it eats crab — the entry's own reported remedy for the venom.

But the detail that made me stop scrolling is the tears. When the al-ayl is bitten, its tears stream down into two little hollows beneath its eye sockets — hollows so deep a finger can fit inside. And there the tears solidify, hardening “like the sun” into stones. Those stones are made into a theriac — an antidote — for snake venoms: the famous “animal bezoar” (al-bad-zahr al-hayawani). The best, the book says, is yellow, and it comes from India, Sind, and Persia. Place it on a snake or scorpion bite and it helps, the book claims; let a poison-drinker hold it in his mouth and it benefits him. “It has an amazing property against poisons.”

Read the framing carefully: the book claims, not the pharmacy. This is medieval belief, reported faithfully — not medical advice.

There's also a haunting vignette tucked into the entry's appendix. Asked about a poet's verse, the scholar al-Zajjaji explains that the al-ayls eat vipers in the summer, grow heated and inflamed from the venom, and then seek water — but when they reach it, they refuse to drink. They circle the water, sniffing at it, because if they drank while the poison still raged in their bellies, the water meeting the venom would kill them. Only when the venom's fury has passed do they drink, safely. The poet compares his own restraint toward his beloved to those thirsting deer, circling the water they desperately need and refusing it — to stay alive.

Antlers: A Pharmacy, a Fishing Rod, and an Alarm Clock

The al-ayl's antlers grow on a strict schedule: nothing until the animal is two years old, then they sprout straight like two stakes. In the third year they branch; the branching keeps increasing until the sixth year, when they stand like two trees on its head. Then — every single year — it casts them off and grows new ones, exposing the fresh antlers to the sun to harden them.

The shedding, the book says, is an inspiration from GOD — because people benefit from the cast antlers in a dozen ways. The medieval properties list (khawass), framed strictly as historical belief: burning the horn as incense repels vermin and every venomous thing; burning and grinding the horn into a tooth-stick clears yellowness and decay from the teeth and strengthens their roots; wearing a piece of the animal keeps a man from sleeping as long as it hangs on him; its blood, drunk, breaks the stone in the bladder; and the antlers ease difficult childbirth, benefit pregnant women, and — burned and licked with honey — expel worms from the belly.

The entry is honest about the al-ayl's build, too: its body is sinew, “neither flesh nor bone,” its horn solid with no hollow — and for all its snake-hunting bravado, the animal itself is “cowardly, in constant dread.” It even flees when it gets too fat, terrified of being caught.

How Hunters Catch the Uncatchable

So how do you catch an animal that jumps off cliffs and eats vipers? Aristotle — cited by name — gives the method: the al-ayl is caught with whistling and singing. It cannot sleep while it hears music, so the hunters distract it with a tune, creep up behind it, and when they see its ears droop — that's the signal. Caught.

And the strangest hunting trick of all involves the al-ayl's oddest habit: it befriends fish. The stag walks down to the seashore to look at the fish, and the fish come up near the land to look at it — a genuine cross-species friendship, per the entry. The hunters know this. So they dress in the al-ayl's skin, the fish come to greet what they think is their friend, and the hunters catch them. Nature documentary material, seven centuries early.

Ibn Awa: The Jackal That Terrifies Chickens More Than the Fox

Quick Answer

Ibn awa (ابن آوى) is the jackal of Hayat al-Hayawan — literally “the son of the howling one,” named for how it seeks out places near the crying of young animals. Chickens fear it even more than the fox: the book says a flock roosting in a tree will tumble to the ground in terror when a jackal simply walks beneath them.

The Son of the Howl

Start with the name, because the jackal's Arabic name is a tiny mystery story. Ibn awa — “son of awa” — and al-Damiri tells you exactly where it came from: the animal was named so because it takes shelter near the howling (عواء) of the young of the tree. Picture it: dusk, the crying of young birds or beasts in the branches, and the jackal slipping into the shadows below, drawn to the sound like a rumor.

Its plural is just as good. You don't say “ibn awas” — you say banat awa, “the daughters of awa.” And it keeps fine company: the weasel (ibn 'irs), the young camel (ibn makhad), the milk-camel (ibn labun) all pluralize the same way — banat 'irs, banat makhad, banat labun, banat awa. A whole grammar club of “sons of” animals.

Four Nicknames for One Sneaky Animal

The old Arabs loved giving animals kunyas — honorific nicknames, the way you'd call a respected man “father of” something. The jackal collected four: Abu Ayyub, Abu Dhi'b (“father of the little wolf”), Abu Ka'b, and Abu Ra'il.

Abu Dhi'b is the honest one. The jackal really is the wolf's scrappy little cousin — same family, smaller package, bigger attitude. When a culture hands an animal four different “father of” titles, you know it's been hanging around human settlements long enough to earn a reputation.

Caught? It Stinks Up the Cage

The entry preserves a couplet of old verse about the jackal, and it's gloriously undignified:

“The jackal is hard to hunt — and when it is caught, it reeks in the cage.”

ريح في قفص — “a stench in the cage.” This is folk observation at its most specific: hunters knew the jackal as nearly uncatchable, and the rare one that got trapped apparently filled its cage with such a smell that it became proverbial. Whether it's fear-musk or pure spite, the book records it straight-faced. You don't get a detail like that from imagination — you get it from someone's grandfather who trapped one once and never forgot the smell.

Chickens Fear It More Than the Fox

Here's the headline fact, and it's a beauty. The book states it flatly: the chickens' fear of the jackal is greater than their fear of the fox.

Why? Because when the jackal passes beneath them — while they're perched up on a tree or a wall — they fall down. All of them. Even a large flock. تساقطت — they drop, they tumble, they rain chickens.

The fox has to climb, sneak, and snatch. The jackal just strolls underneath the roost and the entire flock panics itself right out of the tree. It doesn't hunt the chickens so much as terrify them into delivering themselves. If the fox is a burglar, the jackal is a fire alarm that eats people.

Can You Eat It? The Scholars Argued

Then comes the legal section, and it's a genuine debate — not a footnote, a real scholarly split. The more correct view, according to the heavyweight Shafi'i texts (al-Muharrar, al-Minhaj, the two Hawis), is that eating the jackal is forbidden — because it hunts with its fangs, which puts it in the predator category.

But there's a wrinkle the book is honest about: if someone argued the jackal's fang is too weak to count, then it would rank with the hyena and the fox — and that would be a defensible position. The second opinion, the personal choice of Shaykh Abu Hamid, permits eating it outright.

Imam Ahmad, when asked, gave the blanket rule: everything that tears with its fangs is among the predators. Abu Hanifa and his two companions went with prohibition. So the jackal sits exactly on the legal fault line — fang or no fang — and the book lets the disagreement stand.

The Medieval Medicine Cabinet

The entry closes with its خواص — the “properties” list — and these are pure medieval folk belief. Read them as history, never as advice:

  • Leave the jackal's tongue in a house, and quarreling breaks out among its people. A home-wrecking charm made from one organ.
  • Its meat benefits madness and epilepsy that strikes at the end of the month.
  • Hang its right eye on someone who fears the evil eye, and no eye will harm him.
  • Hang its heart on a person, and he is safe from all predators — by GOD's permission.

The quarrel-tongue is my favorite. Somewhere in the medieval world, someone genuinely believed a jackal's tongue could start a family feud — which tells you less about tongues and more about how badly people wanted an explanation for why their in-laws fought.

It carries four kunyas (Abu Ayyub, Abu Dhi'b, Abu Ka'b, Abu Ra'il), pluralizes as banat awa, and stars in a proverb about stinking up its cage when caught. A small predator with an outsized folk reputation.

The fox must work for its meal; the jackal's mere presence panics the birds into falling. It's the book's own explanation, and it reads like something a farmer told the author.

Al-Babus: The Word That Means “Someone's Little One”

Quick Answer

Al-babus (البابوس) is not really an animal at all — it's a word. Hayat al-Hayawan defines it as “the small one among the children of people and others,” and preserves it through a single line of poetry: a camel yearning for her babus, her little one. The whole entry is two lines long, and it opens the book's bā' chapter.

A New Chapter, A Two-Line Entry

Turn the page past the jackal, and the book changes gears. The alif chapter is done; now begins باب الباء الموحدة — the chapter of the letter bā'. And the very first entry under the new letter is this:

البابوس — “the small one of the children of people and others.”

That's the whole definition. A babus isn't a species. It's anyone's little one: a human baby, a baby animal, the small one of the bunch. The word names a size and a tenderness, not a beast.

This is the shortest entry we've met so far — no anecdotes, no remedies, no legal rulings. Two lines. Rather than invent an animal to fill the silence, I'd rather tell you why those two lines are worth your minute.

The Camel Who Missed Her Baby

Why would a book of animals bother defining a vocabulary word? Because al-Damiri worked the way the great Arabic dictionaries worked — he let a poet prove the word was real. His witness here is Ibn Ahmar, and the line is lovely:

“My camel yearned for her babus in joy — and what is your yearning? Nay, what are you, and the male?”

حنّت قلوصي إلى بابوسها طربا. His qalus — a young she-camel — is calling out for her little one, yearning for her baby with that deep camel groan anyone who's been near camels knows. Then the poet pivots with a shrug: don't ask me about longing — what am I, and the stallion, compared to this?

The tenderness lands on the reread. The word babus existed because someone needed a word for that — a mother camel's cry for her calf — and a poet reached for it the way you'd reach for “baby.” Thirteen centuries later, the feeling is still perfectly legible.

Why a Dictionary Entry Lives in a Book of Animals

There is no animal here. Hayat al-Hayawan is part bestiary, part dictionary, part literature anthology — and al-Damiri collected words the way other men collected specimens. The babus survives in these pages the way a pressed flower survives in a book, because someone loved it enough to keep it.

And that tells you something real about the world this book came from. The camel's longing was worth recording. A poet watched — or imagined — a she-camel crying for her calf, and the language bent to hold the moment, and the great book of animals swept the word up and shelved it between the jackal and the falcon. Natural history, in the medieval sense, included the history of how humans talked about nature.

Small Word, Big Doorway

There's one more thing the babus does: it opens a door. This two-line entry is the first under the letter bā' — which means the long march of the bā' animals starts here. Next in line is the baz (البازي), the falcon — a proper, full, magnificent entry with pride, poetry, and a famous rebuke of a corrupt judge. Every one of those bā' entries walks in behind this tiny word.

So the smallest entry in the book turns out to be a threshold. You step over the word “little one” to get to the falcons. I can't decide if al-Damiri planned that or if the alphabet just did it for him. Either way, I'm glad the baby got the first word.

    Because it shows what Hayat al-Hayawan really is — not just a bestiary but a treasury of the Arabic language, where words were specimens too. Al-Bazi: The Falcon That Flew Into the Sky and Came Back With a Fish

Quick Answer

Al-bazi (البازي) is the falcon of Hayat al-Hayawan — the most arrogant bird in the whole book, named for its pouncing leap. Its entry is bursting with wonders: a falcon that vanished into the sky and came back carrying a fish, a single poem that shamed a judge into resigning, and a famous debate with a rooster about who is truly loyal.

The Most Arrogant Bird Alive

The entry opens with language nerds' delight. The most eloquent form of the name is bazi, with a softened y — then baz, then bazi with a hardened y, all recorded by Ibn Sidah. It's masculine, no disagreement there. Two of them are bazayan, a flock is buzah — like qudah from qadi. And the word suqur, which falconers use for falcons, sparrowhawks, and every hunting bird, is derived from al-buzwan — the leap, the pounce. The falcon is literally named for how it kills.

The old Arabs also handed it three kunyas — honorific nicknames, the way a respected elder gets called “father of” something: Abu al-Ash'ath, Abu al-Bahlul, and Abu Lahiq. And then al-Damiri drops the verdict on its personality: the falcon is “one of the most arrogant of animals and narrowest in temperament.” Imagine a bird so proud the medieval zoologists diagnosed it with attitude.

There's even a strange belief from al-Qazwini tucked in here: people claimed the falcon is only ever female, and that its male comes from an entirely different kind — like kites and sparrowhawks — which is why their shapes differ so much. That's a historical belief, not biology. The medieval world was full of such confident misunderstandings, and the book records them faithfully.

The Fish From the Sky

Here's the headline story. The caliph Harun al-Rashid went out hunting one day and released an ash-grey falcon, a bazi ashhab. The bird kept climbing — higher and higher — until it vanished into the sky. It was gone. Everyone gave up on it. Then, long after hope was lost, it came back — carrying a fish.

The caliph was baffled, so he gathered the scholars and asked them what on earth had happened. A man named Muqatil spoke up: he reported from Ibn Abbas that the air itself is inhabited by nations of creatures of different shapes, and among them are white creatures that hatch things shaped like fish — things with wings, but no feathers. The caliph accepted the explanation and rewarded him handsomely.

A falcon dives into the sky, comes back with a fish, and the scholars of the caliph's court conclude there must be winged fish up there in the blue. It's my favorite kind of medieval reasoning — the observation was real (the bird, the fish), and the theory was gloriously, confidently wrong. Seven centuries later, we can smile at it, but you have to admire the effort: they were trying to explain the world with the tools they had.

The Poem That Made a Judge Quit

The entry then swerves into politics, and it's a scorcher. Abdullah ibn al-Mubarak — a famous scholar who famously combined knowledge with trade, funding his charity work from his own commerce — once heard that a fellow scholar, Ismail ibn 'Ulayyah, had accepted an appointment as a judge under the caliph. Ibn al-Mubarak refused to visit him. Instead he sent a poem that cuts like a knife:

“O you who made knowledge into a falcon, to hunt the wealth of the poor...”

Six verses of it, each sharper than the last: accusing him of scheming for the world and its pleasures, of becoming mad with it after being “the cure for the mad,” of abandoning the hadiths he used to transmit. When Ibn 'Ulayyah read those verses, he didn't argue. He went straight to al-Rashid and kept asking to be relieved of the judgeship — until the caliph let him go.

One poem, one falcon metaphor, one resignation. Poetry in the medieval world was power.

The entry lingers on Ibn al-Mubarak with two more anecdotes, because it clearly loves the man. A man once sneezed near him and forgot to say “alhamdulillah,” so Ibn al-Mubarak politely taught him the etiquette of sneezing right there — and the whole gathering marveled at his manners. And when he once borrowed a pen in Syria, then traveled to Antioch only to realize the pen was still with him — he walked all the way back to Syria, on foot, just to return a pen. They say mercy descends when he is mentioned. He died in the year 181 AH.

The Falcon and the Rooster Debate Loyalty

The proverbs section hides a small masterpiece of a story. A man named Abu Ayyub al-Saqi — Sulayman ibn Abi Mujalid — used to explain his terror of the caliph al-Mansur with a parable about a falcon and a rooster who once debated each other.

The falcon accused the rooster of ingratitude: “I know no creature less loyal than you. You're taken as an egg, raised by people's hands, fed from their palms — and when you're grown, no one can approach you without you fleeing this way and that, shrieking. And me? I'm taken full-grown from the mountains. I'm fed a little, tamed for a day or two, then released to hunt — I fly off alone, catch my prey, and bring it back to my master.”

The rooster's comeback is devastating: “You've missed the point entirely. If you'd seen two falcons on a roasting spit, you'd never go back to your masters again. But I see skewers full of roosters every single day — and I stay with my people anyway. I am more loyal than you.”

Abu Ayyub meant it as an excuse: you people would be just as frightened as I am if you knew al-Mansur the way I do. History proved him right. Al-Mansur had him tortured, seized his wealth, and killed him in 154 AH. The entry adds one more ghost: Abu Ayyub was said to carry a magic oil, smearing it on his eyebrows whenever he entered on the caliph — and “Abu Ayyub's ointment” (dahn Abi Ayyub) became a popular saying among the people.

What Makes a Good Falcon — According to the Falconers

Buried in the middle is the practical falconer's guide, and it's wonderfully specific. There are five kinds of hunting birds: the bazi, the zurq, the bashiq, the baydaq, and the saqr. The bazi has the hottest temperament and the least patience for thirst. It roosts in ancient, dense treetops and deep shade. It's light-winged and fast — and here's a surprise: the females are braver against large birds than the males.

The best bazi, the falconers say, has few feathers and red eyes with sharpness in them — one poet claimed its eyes shine so brightly a traveler at night could navigate by them instead of a lantern. The ideal build: a long neck, a broad chest, wide-set shoulders, a body tapering hard into the tail, long feathered thighs, and thick short forearms. A bazi chick is called a ghatrif. And the falcon became the very symbol of nobility — as the poet said: “many birds fly, but none like the falcon.”

The entry even rates the supporting cast. The bashiq (sparrowhawk, an arabized foreign word, kunya Abu al-Akhidh) is hot-tempered, anxious, sometimes friendly and sometimes wild, strong-willed — and “fit for kings to be served by,” since it hunts the finest game: partridge, pigeons, and turtle-doves. The baydaq catches only sparrows. The 'aqsi is the weakest of the birds of prey, the sparrows sometimes escape it.

Dream a Falcon, Gain a Kingdom

Then there's the dream book, and it's pure medieval fortune-telling. A falcon in your dream means authority — if you're fit for leadership. If it flies from your hand and only its leg remains, your kingdom goes but your fame stays. Leftover feathers mean leftover wealth. Slaughtering a falcon means catching a thief; slaughtering falcons means the death of kings who seize wealth openly. Falcon meat in a dream is the wealth of rulers. And for the common man, falcons mean honor and high position.

So the next time a falcon visits your dreams, the book has it all mapped out.

Does the Falcon Rise Without a Wing?

The Arabs said: “Does the falcon rise without a wing?” — a proverb for cooperation and sticking together, the way a poet reminded his reader: “your cousin is your wing.” And the legal scholars weighed in, too. Most ruled eating birds of prey forbidden, citing the hadith against eating every fanged predator and every taloned bird. Malik and others disagreed, holding no bird forbidden. What everyone agreed on: a falcon is lawful to buy and rent, and its catch is lawful to eat — “whatever it holds for you, eat.”

The Medieval Medicine Cabinet

The entry's خواص — its “properties” list — is pure medieval folk belief, and I'm reporting it as history, never as advice:

  • The falcon's gallbladder, used as kohl, protects the eyes from cataract.
  • If a woman drinks the falcon's droppings mixed with water, it helps her conceive — even if she was barren, the book claims.
  • The bashiq's brain benefits heart palpitations caused by black bile; its gall, as kohl, clears dimness of the eyes.

The conception drink is the kind of claim that makes you wince and keep reading at the same time. That was the medieval world: one part sharp observation, one part magic, bound together in a single leather volume.

Bazil: The Camel That Taught the World to Repay Debts Generously

Quick Answer

Al-bazil (البازل) is the camel whose canine tooth has split — male or female, at exactly eight years old. In Hayat al-Hayawan it's the fully grown adult camel, the animal behind a famous teaching: a borrowed young camel was repaid with a bazil, and the saying went out that “the best of you are the best in repaying.” It even became a metaphor for scholarly seniority.

Eight Years Old, Full Grown

Start with the definition, because it's precision-obsessed in the best way. A bazil is the camel “that has split its canine” — fatara, to split open, like a seed cracking. Male or female, it doesn't matter: if the tooth has split, the camel is eight years old and fully adult.

The Arabs, you see, read a camel's age in its teeth the way we'd read it in an ID card. And the word came with its own plural family: bazal, bazl, and bawazil — as if the language itself couldn't settle on just one way to say “the grown ones.”

This was a culture that didn't guess a camel's age. It named it, graded it, and remembered which tooth meant what.

The Generous Repayment

Now the story that made the bazil famous. The book narrates — via Muslim, from Abu Hurayra — that the Prophet once borrowed a young camel, a bakr, from a man. When it came time to repay the loan, he returned not an equal young camel, but a bazil, an older, stronger, fully grown one. A better animal than the one he had taken.

And then the teaching, the line that traveled fourteen centuries:

“The best of you are the best in repaying.”

The rule was simple: give back better than you took. The borrower upgraded the lender's position out of gratitude, not obligation. In a desert economy where a camel was a truck, a bank account, and a family inheritance all at once, repaying a loan with a superior animal was a serious statement — and it turned into a permanent moral standard: generosity in repayment is the mark of the best people.

I'm reporting this as the book records it — a report carried through Muslim's collection, not inventing a single detail. Here the bazil is the punchline of a lesson about character.

The Scholar Who Knew His Place

The entry closes with a story about scholarly humility, and it's quietly wonderful. Sufyan ibn 'Uyaynah — one of the great early scholars — was once asked about the meaning of a reported teaching: “whoever cleanses himself with stones, let him do it an odd number of times.” He fell silent. They pressed him: “Don't you accept what Malik said about it?” He asked what Malik had said, and they told him: “cleansing with stones is istijmar.”

Instead of answering, Ibn 'Uyaynah quoted a verse of old poetry: when the young ibn labun (the milk-camel, a young one) is penned horn-to-horn with the mighty bazil camels, the young one cannot withstand their charge.

The meaning was clear: next to Malik — the senior, the full-grown, the bazil of scholars — he was just the youngster. He would not venture an opinion where Malik had already spoken.

So the bazil became a metaphor for seniority. A tooth split at eight years turned into a whole vocabulary for knowing when someone outranks you. That's the kind of detail that makes you love this book: zoology one minute, human nature the next.

Baqi'a: The Bird Too Paranoid to Drink Water in Peace 🐦

Quick Answer

The book also gives the name to a wary little bird that never drinks water standing still: it darts right and left mid-sip, as if enemies were waiting at every shore.

A Calamity Walking

Some entries in this book take ten pages. This one takes four lines. And somehow it says more about human nature than the ten-page ones.

Al-Damiri opens with the definition, blunt as a slap: al-baqi'a — الداهية — “the calamity.” A man is called a baqi'a, the book says, when he is a man of daha' — cunning, craftiness, the kind of cleverness that makes rooms go quiet when he walks in.

In English we say someone is “a handful” or “trouble.” The Arabs went bigger: the man himself is the disaster. Not the cause of it — the event. If you ever met someone so sharp that negotiating with him felt like surviving a storm, you know exactly what word his Arabic-speaking contemporaries would have handed him.

Then comes a twist I didn't see coming. The same word is also a bird.

The Bird That Never Stops Moving

Al-Harawi — transmitted from Abu 'Amr — reports that the baqi'a is also a wary bird, a ta'ir hadhir, with one unforgettable habit: when it drinks water, it flies right and left. Yatiiru yamnatan wa yasratan.

It doesn't land, drink, and fly away. It drinks while darting — sip, dart left, sip, dart right. The most vulnerable moment in any animal's day is the moment its head goes down to the water. Predators know this. Prey knows this. And this little bird's solution is paranoia as a lifestyle: never present a still target, not even for a drink.

I have to admit, I love this bird. There's something deeply relatable about refusing to relax even at the watering hole.

One Word, One Philosophy of Watchfulness

But one word covers both the cunning man and the paranoid bird because both survive by never being where you expect. The baqi'a-man is the one you can't trap, can't outmaneuver, can't read — his mind is always one sip ahead of yours. The baqi'a-bird is the one you can't catch — its body is always one dart ahead of the hawk. Watchfulness is the common thread: one watches with his mind, the other with its wings.

The entry closes with two old reports, and they're the best part. In what the book calls “the hadith of the tribes,” 'Ali says to Abu Bakr: “You have come across a baqi'a among the Bedouins.” No name, no description — just the word, and the warning inside it. Whoever this Bedouin was, 'Ali's message was clear: careful. This one is clever. He is a calamity walking.

And in another report: “So I opened conversation with him — and behold, he was a baqi'a.” I love that little scene. Someone sits down for a chat, starts talking, and mid-conversation realizes who he's dealing with. You can almost see the mental recalculation: oh. Oh. This is one of those.

Four lines: a calamity, a cunning man, a paranoid bird, and a conversation that changed tone halfway through. Al-Damiri filed an entire philosophy of watchfulness under a single word — and seven centuries later, it still works. We all know a baqi'a. Some of us are married to one.

Balam: The Mystery Word Behind Paradise's Feast of Bull and Whale 🐂

Quick Answer

In Hayat al-Hayawan, balam (بالام) is a mystery word from a famous report found in both al-Bukhari and Muslim: on the Day of Judgment, the people of Paradise will feast on “balam and nun” — a bull and a whale — with seventy thousand eating from the excess of their livers. Scholars debated the word for centuries; the likeliest answer is that it's Hebrew.

The Strangest Dinner Invitation Ever Recorded

The entry opens with a scene, not a definition. A report carried in both al-Bukhari and Muslim describes the Day of Resurrection: the earth will become a single loaf of bread, which the Almighty will turn over in His Hand the way a traveler flips his loaf on a journey — a welcoming feast for the people of Paradise.

Then a Jewish man steps forward. “May the Merciful bless you, Abu al-Qasim,” he says. “Shall I tell you about the feast of the people of Paradise on that Day?” “Yes,” comes the answer. “The earth will be a single loaf — just as the Messenger of GOD said.” The Prophet looks at his companions, laughs until his molars show, and asks: “Shall I tell you what they'll eat with it?” “Yes.” “Balam and nun.” “What are those?” “A bull and a fish. From the excess of their two livers, seventy thousand will eat.”

Seventy thousand from the livers — this is a feast that doesn't obey biology, and it isn't trying to.

The Interrogation

Muslim's version gives the same story with more interrogation-room energy. A Jewish scholar comes to the Prophet with a list of questions, each one a test. Where will people be when the earth and heavens are changed? “In a darkness, short of the Gathering.” Who crosses first? “The poor emigrants.” What is their welcoming gift in Paradise? “The excess of the nun's liver.” What comes after? “The bull of Paradise — the one that grazed at its edges — will be slaughtered for them.” And to drink? “From a spring in it called Salsabil.”

Every answer lands. “You have spoken the truth,” the questioner says, again and again, until finally: “You are indeed a prophet.” And when he's gone, the Prophet tells his companions something quietly stunning: “He asked me about what he asked, and I had no knowledge of any of it — until GOD, the Mighty, gave it to me.”

Al-Bukhari adds one more detail, via Anas: the questioner was Abdullah ibn Salam — the famous Jewish scholar of Medina who accepted Islam. “So the hadith came explained,” al-Damiri notes, as if closing a case file.

A Hebrew Word for a Bull?

Then the entry turns detective, and honestly this is my favorite part. Al-nun is easy: it's the fish, the whale — which is why Jonah is called Dhu al-Nun, the Man of the Fish. But balam? Scholars, the book admits, “attempted explanations that were not satisfactory.” The leading theory, quoted from al-Nihaya: it's probably a Hebrew word.

Al-Khattabi offered a wilder theory: maybe the questioner was being deliberately cryptic, cutting the spelling and transposing letters — what he really said was la'y, the wild bull, and some narrator miscopied the letter ya' as a ba'. “This is the closest that occurs to me,” he says — and al-Damiri, with a scholar's shrug across seven centuries, concludes: the sound view is that it's a Hebrew word. Some mysteries don't get solved. They get filed.

The 500-Cubit Monster Next Door

The entry doesn't stop at the feast. It introduces al-bal (البال): a fish of the greatest ocean, fifty cubits long, called 'anbar — the ambergris fish, the sperm whale. Not an Arabic word, the dictionaries insist; “as if Arabized.”

Then al-Qazwini walks in with the tall tale of the century: the bal is five hundred cubits long or more, surfacing only sometimes, the tip of its wing like a great sail. Sailors fear it beyond all fear — when they sense it, they beat drums to drive it off. And here's the detail that made me grin. When the bal turns on the creatures of the sea, GOD sends a fish about a cubit long that clamps onto its ear — and the monster has no escape from the minnow. It dives for the sea floor and beats the earth with its head until it dies, then floats on the water like a great mountain. The people of Zanj watch for the corpses, drag them ashore with hooks, split the bellies, and pull out the ambergris.

From a mystery word for a heavenly bull to a sea monster defeated by a one-cubit fish with an ear fetish — all under one entry. That's Hayat al-Hayawan for you. It never files anything the way you'd expect, and that's exactly why I keep reading.

First, the name. Al-babr — two bā's, the first with a fatha, the second with a kasra. Get it wrong and you've lost the whole animal. It's a beast of prey, and here's the first strange detail: it “rivals the lion in running” — in running, not in fighting. Not a war with the lion. A race. A tiger that doesn't brawl with the king of beasts; it outruns him.

It answers to other names — al-barid, al-faraniq — and it's Indian, an Arabized import, built something like the jackal. Then the genealogy, which reads like the start of a fable: it's said to be born of zabarqan and a lioness. And the part that made me stop and reread: the female conceives from the wind. Not as a metaphor. From the wind. That, the book says, is why its running is like the wind's running — and why no hunter on earth can catch it.

The Glass-Bottle Heist

You don't hunt the mother. You steal.

Thieves slip in and snatch the cubs, seal them into glass flasks and jars, and gallop off on the fastest horses they own. And when the father catches up — because he always catches up — they toss him one of the bottles. He stops. He stares at his cub floating behind the glass, puzzling over how to get it out, and while he's standing there baffled, the rest of the crew vanishes with the remaining cubs. The stolen ones grow up among children, tame, used to people. A tiger father so bewildered by a glass bottle that he forgets the chase. The book presents this as fieldcraft, not folklore.

Then the entry pivots, and the babr becomes something stranger than a beast: a security guard. It loves the camphor tree — haunts it constantly. While the babr is at the tree, nobody can take a single piece of camphor. Nobody even tries. But it leaves at a known, fixed time, and the people of those parts know the schedule. They wait. When the guard goes off duty, they walk in and harvest. A wild tiger working a timed shift — and the locals planning their whole trade around its timetable. I love that the book treats this as logistics, not magic.

The jurists ruled its meat forbidden — it strengthens itself with its canine; hunter's logic. The khawass section goes further, and remember: this is what the book claims, not medical advice. Its heel bone, tied to the forearm, supposedly keeps the bearer from ever tiring, even at twenty parasangs a day. And sitting on its skin was said to clear the skin condition called hubb al-qara'.

As for what it looks like: the Rabi' al-Abrar describes it as the size of a great lion, white gleaming with yellow and black stripes — which sounds exactly like a tiger to me. Aristotle, quoted at the entry's end, puts this fearsome beast in Abyssinia and nowhere else. Somewhere between the wind, the glass bottles, and the camphor schedule, the babr walked off the page. I keep thinking about that father, nose to the glass, watching his cub float away.

The Parrot That Spies for Kings: Medieval Tales of Talking Parrots 🦜

Quick Answer

In Hayat al-Hayawan, the parrot (الببغاء) is a gentle, sharp-witted green bird that mimics human speech — kings kept them to repeat news they overheard, and Aristotle taught one to talk using a mirror. The entry also records a snowy-white parrot with a black beak and pistachio crest, gifted to a prince: a color extinct in the author's time.

A Bird Named by Its Consonants

The name is its own pronunciation guide. Al-babghā' — the parrot's name in the book — three bā's, the first and third with fathas, the second silent, then a ghayn. The book calls this green bird al-durra. It's about the size of a dove, and people keep it the way they keep a peacock — except with a peacock you're paying for the voice and the color, and with the parrot you're paying for the voice alone.

Because this bird talks. Not chirps — talks. The entry calls it gentle in character and piercing in understanding, with real power to mimic sounds and take instruction. And here's the line that made me grin: kings and great men keep parrots so the birds will repeat the news they hear. A feathered surveillance device. Your parrot sits through the council meeting and blabs it all back at dinner. In the medieval palace, loose lips didn't sink ships — loose beaks did.

Teaching a Parrot to Talk: Aristotle's Mirror Trick

Aristotle's method: take a mirror, set it before the parrot so it sees its own reflection, then speak from behind the mirror and repeat. The bird, convinced another bird is talking to it, answers back. The oldest language-lab trick on record — a mirror and patience. And it crossed languages: Ibn al-Faqih writes that on Ranj island he saw parrots — red, white, yellow — repeating speech in whatever language it came at them in. Any language.

Then the entry breaks into poetry, and the poem is a love letter. Abu Ishaq al-Sabi's ode calls her pretty of morning, eloquent of speech — ranked among the birds, yet her tongue suggests a human. She reports news to her owner, uncovers secrets, mute herself but listening, repeating all she hears. An honored guest, fed walnuts and rice, beak like a pearl, eyes like gems that shine in light and darkness, in a green robe like a young maiden. And the closing image lands like a stone: “We cage her though she has no fault — only from excess of love.”

White as Snow, and Gone Forever

Then a ghost. A white parrot was once gifted to Mu'izz al-Dawla ibn Buyeh — snow-white body, black beak, black feet, a pistachio tuft on its head. Try to picture it: the strangest, most beautiful bird in the prince's court. And the book's quiet verdict: all the colors are gone now except the green. The white parrot existed — and then it didn't. Somewhere a prince's bird died, and a color vanished from the world.

A Funeral Letter and a Preaching Parrot

Ibn Khallikan preserves the best anecdote: Ahmad ibn Yusuf wrote to a friend whose parrot had died — a friend whose brother, Abd al-Hamid, had a habit of never showing up. “Death has taken your parrot,” the letter jokes, “and skipped your brother Abd al-Hamid — who would have been the more fitting and deserving candidate.” Grief with a punchline. Medieval friendship in a single paragraph.

And stranger still: al-Zamakhshari records a parrot that said, “Woe to him whose concern is this world.” Somewhere in the medieval world, someone trained a bird to preach — and a scholar wrote it down. I keep wondering what the sermons sounded like.

What the Jurists and Dream-Readers Said

The jurists argued over the bird the way they argue over everything. Al-Rafi'i ruled its meat forbidden — foul flesh. Others said lawful: it eats good foods, it's not venomous, not clawed. Al-Mutawalli settled the practical question: you may rent a parrot for the company of its voice. The khawass section, as always, is where things get weird — the book claims eating its tongue makes a man eloquent and bold in speech, its gallbladder eaten heavyens the tongue, its blood dried and ground and sprinkled between two friends breeds enmity, and its droppings mixed with unripe-grape juice make a kohl for eye ailments. Historical beliefs, every one — filed here as curiosities, not advice. And in dreams? The parrot is a bad-omened lying man. Or a philosopher. Its chick: a philosopher's son. Or — the entry's last word — a young slave girl, or an orphan boy.

Al-bajj: one of the water birds. And the mention of the whole genus will come, GOD willing, in the chapter of ṭā'.

That is it. That is the entire al-bajj entry in Hayat al-Hayawan. One sentence. The shortest entries in this book sometimes hide the wildest stories — but not this one. This one is a signpost, and it points forward.

Writers of every age know the power of “more on this later.”

I like to imagine a reader in the 1400s, lamplight, the weight of the volume on his knees, reaching the al-bajj line and making a choice: flip ahead to the ṭā' chapter right now, or savor the wait. Some habits do not change in seven centuries. I am the kind who would flip.

So here is the honest ledger, and I will not pad it. The entry confirms one fact about al-bajj: it is a water bird — “min ṭayr al-mā'.” Everything else is the promise attached to the fact: the full treatment of the genus lives in the ṭā' chapter, and it is coming.

Quick Answer

That is the entire entry in Hayat al-Hayawan — one line, with the word's letters spelled out one by one so no scribe can garble it. Behind it stands the wild cow itself: the desert bovine the classical dictionaries call بقر الوحش.

The Whole Entry, Quoted in Full

The entry reads, complete and entire:

بالباء الموحدة والزاي والجيم: ولد البقرة الوحشية.

“With bā', zāy, and jīm: the calf of the wild cow.”

That is it. Five words of definition after the spelling. If you were hoping for a story, I understand — this book has trained me to expect ten pages. But wait. This little line is doing more work than it looks like.

Why Spell Out the Letters?

The first thing al-Damiri does is not define the word — he spells it: bil-bā' al-muwaḥḥada, wal-zāy, wal-jīm. Bā', zāy, jīm. Why? Because Arabic script has traps. A word you have never seen can be misread three ways, and al-Damiri knows his reader may never have met this one. Strip the dots and ب could be ت, ث, ن, or ي. A hurried scribe turns ز into ر and ج into ح without noticing.

By naming each letter outright, he pins the word down like a butterfly on a board: this exact word, no other. Seven centuries of hand-copying later, the word arrives intact. That is not fussiness. That is engineering.

And the spelling tells you something about this book's ambition. This is not a greatest-hits collection. It is a work that refuses to leave any animal word behind — even one with no anecdote attached, even one nobody would miss. The ب chapter has room for the tiger, the falcon, and the parrot — and room for a single obscure word meaning “wild cow's calf,” spelled out letter by letter.

The Wild Cow Behind the Word

So what is the البقرة الوحشية — the “wild cow”? The classical dictionaries describe بقر الوحش as wild oxen, bulls and cows — the desert bovines, not farm animals. The oryx-like herds of the open country, the kind the old poets put beside the most beautiful things they knew. (Keeping it honest: that is what the dictionaries say about the wild cow generally. Al-Damiri's entry adds nothing beyond the calf's name — and does not pretend to.)

There is something touching about a language keeping a special word for the wild cow's calf. English does not — we say “calf” and move on. Old Arabic kept a whole vocabulary of the young: the جحش for the donkey's foal, the عجل for the cow's, and here, the بخزج for the wild cow's. A language built by people who watched herds the way sailors watch weather. Each young animal got its own word.

What the Bakhzaj Line Really Carries

I keep coming back to those five words — ولد البقرة الوحشية, “the calf of the wild cow.” Ordinary words, every one. But together they preserve something: proof that somewhere, someone needed this word. A hunter at the waterhole describing what he saw. A poet reaching for exactly the right young animal. A lexicographer refusing to let a real word die.

Al-Damiri could have skipped it. Nobody would have noticed. Instead he gave it an entry, spelled its letters, and handed it to the future. Seven centuries later, here we are, reading it. The calf of the wild cow — still named, still safe. That is not nothing. That is the whole point of a book like this.

One thing nobody argued about: these camels are tall. جمال طوال الأعناق — “camels with long necks.” The Bactrian, the two-humped breed of Central Asia, towers over the one-humped Arabian camel, and you can feel al-Damiri pointing at it like a man in a market: that one. The long-necked one.

The camel is a footnote to a legal ruling. But picture it: a voyage by sea, and someone had stolen a two-humped camel — grand theft, camel-sized. The law said: not here, not now.

Medieval readers heard that as a warning about towering, padded hairstyles. The bakht's humps were the tallest, most unmistakable silhouette in the animal kingdom — everyone knew exactly what the image meant. Another version, in al-Mustadrak, sharpens the scene: men riding on plush saddle pads, arriving at the mosque doors while their women are “clad yet naked,” their heads like the humps of lean bakht camels. “Curse them,” the report says, “for they are cursed.”

I read this and thought: a camel's hump as a fashion crime. Fourteen centuries later, the image still lands.

The entry ends on a softer note. From grammar wars to theft to prophecy to Paradise — all inside one entry, all hung on the humps of one tall camel. The bakht earned its place in this book the way the great animals always do: not by being ordinary, but by being unforgettable.

A man with almost nothing buys the costliest offering there is, and his defense is a line of scripture.

The First Badana in History

Ilyas ibn Mudar was the first man, the book says, to dedicate badan to the Sacred House — and the first to set up the Station of Abraham after the flood of Noah's time had scattered it. The Arabs honored him for it until the day he died.

Then his wife, Khindif, grieved like nobody else in this book. She forbade herself men and perfume, and wandered homeless until she died of sorrow. A camel offering, a station stone, and a widow's Thursdays. History and heartbreak in one entry.

Al-Badhj: The Little Lamb Behind the Bedouin's Dream Dinner and the Proverb of Shame 🐑

Quick Answer

Al-badhj (البذج) is the Arabic word for a baby lamb — a Persian loanword for the weakest of the flock. In the medieval book Hayat al-Hayawan it became the symbol of utter meekness: a dying Bedouin's dream dinner, a proverb for the humiliated, and the shape of a ruined soul on Judgment Day.

A Persian Word for the Weakest Lamb

The entry starts with the letter itself: البذج, with the dotted dhāl. It means one of the children of sheep — the lamb — the same way the 'atūd is the young billy among goats. Its plural is bidjān.

And the word wasn't even Arabic to begin with. The book notes it is a Persian word the Arabs adopted. A whole language's symbol of meekness was imported.

There is an old verse the book quotes, and it is brutally funny: “Our neighbor woman has perished from the hamaj... and if she gets hungry she eats an 'atūd or a badhj.” Al-Jawhari, the dictionary man, explains: by “al-hamaj” the poet meant bad management of livelihood. She let her affairs rot until she is down to eating her own young livestock — kid goats and baby lambs. It is a joke about ruin, and the badhj is the punchline: when you are eating the babies, you are done.

The Judgment-Day Badhj

This is where the little lamb gets heavy. A report comes through Ibn al-Mubarak, from Isma'il ibn Muslim, from al-Hasan and Qatada, from Anas: “A man will come out of the Fire like a badhj, his joints trembling.”

Not a roaring lion. Not a raging bull. A baby lamb, shaking.

And there is a longer scene, through the same chain. On the Day of Judgment a man is brought “like a badhj from humiliation” and made to stand before GOD. GOD says to him: “I gave you. I granted you. I blessed you. What did you do?” And the man says: “Lord, I gathered it and grew it and left it more than it was — send me back and I'll bring it all to You.”

GOD answers: “Show Me what you sent forward.”

And he is a servant who sent forward nothing good. So he is taken to the Fire.

Ibn al-Arabi al-Maliki wrote it down in his Siraj al-Muridin and graded it sound among the marasil of al-Hasan; al-Mundhiri noted that al-Tirmidhi transmitted it too. The commentator adds the point plainly: he was likened to the badhj because of the humiliation and contempt he brings with him. The lamb's meekness is the man's condition — small, stripped, nothing to stand on.

“I Am the Best Divider”

A second report, in the Musnad of Abu Ya'la from Anas, brings the badhj back to the same scene. The son of Adam is brought on the Day of Judgment like a badhj from humiliation, and GOD says: “I am the best divider. O son of Adam, look at your deed — the one you did for Me, and I will reward you for it. And look at your deed — the one you did for someone else — for your reward is on the one you did it for.”

Abu Nu'aym, the book notes, transmitted the same report in the biography of al-Rabi' ibn Sabih.

Twice in one entry the badhj walks into the end of the world. The weakest lamb, carrying the heaviest image.

The Death of Abu Kharija

Then the entry turns, completely, into a comedy. They found a Bedouin clinging to the curtains of the Ka'ba, praying: “O GOD, make me die the death of Abu Kharija.”

They asked him: how did Abu Kharija die?

He said: “He ate a badhj, drank from a mash'al, and slept in the sun — and he met GOD full, quenched, and warm.” The mash'al, the book explains, is a vessel in which drink is soaked.

Read that again. A whole lamb. His fill of drink. A nap in the warm sun. And that, to this Bedouin, is the perfect death — so perfect he holds the Ka'ba's curtains and asks GOD for it. Full belly, quenched thirst, warm sleep. Honestly? Hard to argue.

More Humbled Than a Badhj

The entry closes with a proverb: “So-and-so is more humbled than a badhj” — because, the book says, it is the weakest of the lambs.

That is the badhj's whole legacy in medieval Arabic: not the cute lamb, not the farmyard darling. It is the measure of smallness. The thing you compare broken men to.

  1. What does “badhj” actually mean?

    With the dotted dhāl (ذ), al-badhj is one of the children of sheep — the baby lamb — the same way the 'atūd is the young billy among goats. Its plural is bidjān. The word is a Persian loanword the Arabs adopted, and the book's proverb calls it “the weakest of the lambs,” which is why it became Arabic's walking symbol of meekness.

  2. Who was Abu Kharija, and why did the Bedouin want his death?

    In the story, Abu Kharija was a man who died the perfect desert death: he ate a whole young lamb, drank his fill from a soaking-vessel, and slept in the warm sun — meeting GOD “full, quenched, and warm.” A Bedouin found clinging to the Ka'ba's curtains made it his prayer: O GOD, let me die that way.

  3. Why is a ruined soul compared to a badhj on Judgment Day?

    Two reports in the entry bring the sinner before GOD “like a badhj from humiliation” — small, trembling, stripped of pride. One has a man leaving the Fire with shaking joints; the other has him pleading for another chance, until GOD says: “Show Me what you sent forward.” He sent nothing good — so the meekness of the lamb becomes the measure of his failure.

Al-Buraq: The Lightning Steed Whose One Stride Touched the Seven Heavens ✨

Quick Answer

Al-Buraq (البراق) is the white heavenly steed that carried the Prophet Muhammad on the Night Journey. Smaller than a mule and bigger than a donkey, named for lightning, it placed each stride at the farthest edge of its gaze — crossing from earth to heaven in one stride, the seven heavens in seven — and will carry him again on Judgment Day.

Named for Lightning

The entry opens with the headline fact: al-Buraq is the beast the Prophet rode on the Night of the Isra' — and the book adds that the prophets rode it before him. Its name comes from al-barq, lightning flashing in the clouds.

That speed shows up in another famous image: the hadith of crossing the Sirat, where people cross “some like lightning, some like a storm wind, some like a noble horse.” The Buraq is lightning's cousin — the fastest thing in the sky, made into a mount.

Smaller Than a Mule, Bigger Than a Donkey

The Sahih description is almost disappointingly plain until you do the math: a beast smaller than a mule and bigger than a donkey, white, and it “places its stride at the farthest edge of its gaze.”

The farthest edge of its gaze. Wherever it can see, it lands. From that single detail the book draws the geometry: he was taken from earth to heaven in one stride — and through the seven heavens in seven strides.

And then the entry does something I love: it turns the Buraq into a debate weapon. Theologians had scoffed that Bilqis's throne could not be brought to Solomon in a single moment — too far, too fast. The book's reply: if a steed can stride to heaven, a throne can arrive in an instant. Checkmate, by Buraq.

“Are You Not Ashamed, O Buraq?”

Al-Suhayli records the moment the Prophet mounted: Gabriel rebuked the steed — “Are you not ashamed, O Buraq? No servant has ridden you more honored to GOD than Muhammad.”

Why would the Buraq need scolding? Ibn Battal's answer is almost tender: the Buraq had been waiting. It was the long separation from the prophets — the centuries between Jesus and Muhammad — that made it restless. A creature that had not carried a worthy rider in generations, suddenly startled by one.

I like that image: the fastest creature in creation, fidgeting with nerves after a centuries-long wait.

Why Not a Horse?

Here is the odd detail: the Buraq came in the form of a mule, not a horse. Why would heaven send the workmanlike mule instead of the noble horse? The book offers the wisdom behind it: to signal that this journey was in peace and safety, not war and fear. A horse means battle; a mule means a calm road.

Or — the grander reading — to make the miracle bigger. Wondrous speed in a beast not famed for speed is a louder proof than speed in a racehorse. The miracle is the point, not the animal.

(Someone raised the obvious objection: the Prophet rode a mule in battle too. The answer: that was to prove his prophethood and bravery. Different ride, different message.)

Al-Nawawi, the careful scholar, adds a footnote of honesty: the claim that all the prophets rode it needs a sound transmission. The shared-riding theory needed proof, and he said so.

White Steed, Gray Mule

One more color note: the Buraq was white; the Prophet's own mule was grayish, mostly white. The book reads that as a sign — he was singled out with the noblest of colors.

And here is a story I had not heard before: Ibrahim used to visit his son Ismail on the Buraq. When he brought Hagar and Ismail to the Sacred House, all three of them — Ibrahim, Ismail, Hagar — rode it. A family trip, on lightning.

The Return Trip

Abu al-Qasim al-Isfahani asks the puzzle: why did the Buraq carry him up to heaven but not bring him back down? His answer: it ascended with him to display his honor — and it did not descend with him, to display GOD's power. Or, in the book's other phrasing, the ascent itself pointed to the descent, the way the Qur'an's “coats to protect you from the heat” means also the cold, and “in His hand is the good” means also evil — Arabic saying half and meaning all.

Hudhayfa adds the quiet footnote: “He did not leave the Buraq's back until he returned.”

One disputed report, in al-Mustadrak, flips the picture — “I was brought the Buraq, and I rode behind Gabriel.” The book flags it honestly: its narrator, Abu Hamza Maymun al-A'war, is a man the scholars differed about.

Judgment Day

The Buraq is not done. On the Day of Judgment, the book says, the Prophet alone among the prophets will ride it — meeting the believers of his nation at the Gathering, with Fatima raised before him on his she-camel al-'Adba'. Salih gets his mount back too: GOD raises his she-camel, he milks it, drinks with the believers, then rides it to the Standing-place as it bellows. A man asked the Prophet: so you, on that day, on al-'Adba'? “My daughter Fatima will be gathered on that one,” he said, “and I will be gathered on the Buraq — I am singled out with it among the prophets.”

When Was the Night Journey?

Even the date was argued. Ibn al-Athir's verdict: Monday night, the 27th of Rabi' al-Awwal, one year before the Hijra. Al-Nawawi agreed in his commentary on Muslim — but in his fatwas, in the Book of Prayer, he said Rabi' al-Akhir. The Sirat al-Rawda said Rajab. And it happened at night, the book notes, so the distinction would show: between the daytime companion of the King and the nighttime one.

There is one more thing about this entry I have to mention, because it is pure al-Damiri: after the Buraq's facts, the entry quietly swells into the Prophet's entire biography — the Hijra, the battles, the marriages — folded inside an animal's entry. One beast, and suddenly the whole world comes along for the ride. That is this book. You open it for a lamb and find Judgment Day; you open it for a steed and get history.

Frequently Asked Questions

  1. What did the Buraq look like?

    White, smaller than a mule and bigger than a donkey — deliberately not a war-horse, the book says, to signal the Night Journey was one of peace and safety, not battle. It placed each stride at the farthest edge of its gaze, from which the book infers it crossed from earth to heaven in a single stride and through the seven heavens in seven strides.

  2. Did Gabriel ride with the Prophet on the Buraq?

    The scholars disagreed. Some said yes, Gabriel was his riding companion; the book's preferred view is no — the Night Journey was the Prophet's exclusive honor. A disputed report has it the other way round, with the Prophet riding behind Gabriel, but the book flags its narrator as contested by the scholars.

  3. Will the Buraq appear on Judgment Day?

    Yes — the book says the Prophet alone among the prophets will ride the Buraq on that day, meeting the believers at the Gathering with Fatima raised before him on his she-camel al-'Adba'. Salih gets his she-camel back too — GOD raises it, he milks it and drinks, then rides it to the Standing-place as it bellows.

Barghan: The Wild Cow's Calf You Can Pronounce Two Ways 🐂

Quick Answer

Al-barghan (البرغن) is the Arabic word for the calf of the wild cow — and the whole entry in Hayat al-Hayawan is one line. The book's only concern is pronunciation: read it with fatha (bar-ghan) or damma (bur-ghun). Both are correct, and al-Damiri says so outright.

The Whole Entry, Quoted in Full

Here is the entry, complete:

بفتح الباء والغين المعجمة وضمهما ولد البقرة الوحشية.

“With the opening of the bā' and the dotted ghayn, and their joining: the calf of the wild cow.”

Eleven words. The entire entry. And — I checked twice — that is all there is. No story, no poem, no hunting anecdote. Just a word, its two readings, and what it means.

But hold on. Read it again, because this line is doing something the neighboring entries don't.

Two Readings, Both Official

Most of this book's entries pin a word down to ONE form. Not this one. Al-Damiri gives you options: فتح — open the bā' and the ghayn with a fatha, and you get bar-ghan. ضم — join them with a damma, and you get bur-ghun. Two pronunciations, both blessed by the book.

I love this. A 700-year-old encyclopedia that could have picked a winner and instead said: say it either way, you're right. This is not sloppiness — it is the book's ear for living speech. People in different tents said it differently, and the dictionary recorded both.

The Dotted Ghayn

Notice he doesn't just say “ghayn.” He says الغين المعجمة — the DOTTED ghayn. Because in Arabic script, غ and ع are the same shape; the only difference is the dot. One dot separates a gargling sound from a throaty one, and with it, one word from a totally different word.

Al-Damiri has seen what a missing dot can do to a manuscript. Yesterday's entry, al-bakhzaj, spelled out its letters one by one for exactly this reason. Here he is more economical: just the dot, thank you. The reader who knows the ghayn is dotted will never misread this word. Precision with minimum ink.

The Second Calf of the Wild Cow

Here is the part that made me grin. This is the SECOND word for the wild cow's calf in the ب chapter. Yesterday's entry, al-bakhzaj (البخزج), meant exactly the same thing: ولد البقرة الوحشية. Word for word, the same definition.

Two words. Same calf. One defined by spelling out its letters, one by declaring its two vowels. The book refused to drop either.

Why keep both? Because someone, somewhere, actually said both. The desert was big and the dialects differed, and al-Damiri's project was never “one true word” — it was every real word, kept safe. A hunter's word from one valley, a poet's word from another, each with its own entry, each pronounced the way its people pronounced it.

The Wild Cow Behind Both Words

The البقرة الوحشية — the wild cow — is what the classical dictionaries call بقر الوحش: the desert bovines, wild cattle and not farm animals, the oryx-like herds the old poets put next to the most beautiful things they knew. (That is the dictionaries' picture of the wild cow generally; al-Damiri's two calf-entries add nothing to it — and honestly say so.)

Old Arabic kept a whole nursery of words for the young: the جحش for the donkey's foal, the عجل for the tame cow's calf — and not one but two words, bakhzaj and barghan, for the wild cow's. A language built by people who watched herds the way sailors watch weather.

One Line, Then the Flea

And here is my favorite detail: this entry sits at the very top of page 177 — and the very next entry is البرغوث, the flea. The book goes from the calf of the wild cow, an animal you could ride, to a bloodsucking speck you can barely see, in the space of one line break.

That is Hayat al-Hayawan in miniature: no hierarchy, no favorites. The calf gets eleven words. The flea, as it happens, gets pages. Every creature weighed on its own scale.

So say it however you like — bar-ghan or bur-ghun. The book says you're right either way. Seven centuries later, the calf of the wild cow is still named twice, still safe.

Frequently Asked Questions

  1. What does al-barghan mean?

    Al-barghan (البرغن) is the Arabic word for the calf of the wild cow — the young of the wild bovine (بقر الوحش). Hayat al-Hayawan defines it in a single line, noting that the bā' and the dotted ghayn may take either fatha or damma, giving two valid readings.

  2. How do you pronounce it — bar-ghan or bur-ghun?

    Both, according to the entry itself: بفتح الباء والغين المعجمة وضمهما — “with the opening of the bā' and the dotted ghayn, and their joining.” Fatha gives bar-ghan; damma gives bur-ghun. The book records both readings as valid, reflecting how different speakers said the word.

  3. Is al-barghan the same as al-bakhzaj?

    They share the exact same definition — “the calf of the wild cow” (ولد البقرة الوحشية). Both sit in the book's ب chapter as separate entries: al-bakhzaj defined by spelling out its letters, al-barghan by its two vowel readings. Two dialect words for the same young animal, each kept.

Al-Barghuth: The Tiny Flea the Prophet Wouldn't Let Anyone Curse

Quick Answer

Al-barghuth (البرغوث) is the flea of the medieval Arabic book Hayat al-Hayawan — a champion jumper that leaps backward to watch its hunter, shaped “like an elephant” with tusks and a trunk, tangled in Arabic grammar debates, and famous for waking a prophet for the Fajr prayer. The Prophet forbade cursing it — while still recommending that it be killed.

The Bug That Broke Arabic Grammar

The entry opens, of all places, in a grammar fight. There is an old Arabic phrase, أكلوني البراغيث — “the fleas ate me” — from the dialect of the Tayyi' tribe, where the verb takes a plural marker before the plural subject even arrives. Grammarians argued over it for centuries; the book notes it was even used to parse Quranic verses, until Sibawayh ruled flatly: this construction is not in the Quran — in وأسروا النجوى, the pronoun is the subject and الذين is a substitute for it.

A flea so annoying it bent the rules of the language. The book adds that the dammah reading of its name — al-burghūth — is more famous than the kasrah one, as if even the vowels couldn't agree.

Father of the Leaper

The flea's kunyas — its honorary nicknames — say everything: Abu Tafir, Abu 'Adi, and the best of them, Abu al-Waththab — “Father of the Leaper.” It also goes by Tamir ibn Tamir, “Leaper son of Leaper.”

And here is the detail I love most. The flea, the book says, jumps BACKWARD — deliberately — so it can see who is hunting it. If it jumped forward, its death would come sooner. Out of GOD's kindness to it, the tiniest jumper got a rear-view escape plan.

The wonders pile up: al-Jahiz reported from Yahya al-Barmaki that flight can happen to the flea just as it happens to ants; it mates long, lays eggs, and hatches after being born; it first grows out of dust, especially in dark places; its season is late winter into early spring; it is humpbacked and ever-leaping. And the kicker: it is said to be “in the form of the elephant — with tusks it bites with and a trunk it sucks with.” A flea. Shaped like an elephant. Tusks for biting, a trunk for drinking your blood.

The Prophet's Alarm Clock

Then the entry turns reverent. The ruling: eating the flea is forbidden, and killing it is recommended — for the pilgrim in ihram and everyone else. But cursing it? Never.

The book brings the reports. Anas narrated — through Ahmad, al-Bazzar, al-Bukhari's Book of Manners, and al-Tabarani — that the Prophet heard a man cursing a flea and said: “Do not curse it, for it woke a prophet for the Fajr prayer.” Another version, from 'Ali: their caravan camped, fleas tormented them, they cursed the fleas — and the Prophet said: “Do not curse them. What an excellent beast — it woke you for the remembrance of GOD.”

Kill it, yes. Curse it, no. The flea that ruins your sleep might be the same one that gets you up for prayer.

A mercy-rule follows that only a jurist could love: a little flea-blood on your clothes or body is forgiven — everyone gets bitten, avoidance is impossible. The scholars agreed to overlook it unless it gets excessive, and al-Nawawi's soundest view forgives even much of it, outright. The same for every bloodless pest: bugs, mosquitoes, and their like.

The Persian-Cane Flea Trap

Now the entry's strangest treasure — filed, as always, as a historical belief, not advice. A “proven, subtle secret” for a flea-infested house: take a Persian cane, smear it with donkey's milk and billy-goat fat, and plant it in the middle of the house. Then say twenty-five times: “I adjure you, O fleas — you are a troop of GOD's troops from the time of 'Ad and Thamud — I adjure you by the Creator of existence, the One, the Eternal, the Worshipped: gather to this stick. And you have upon me covenants and promises that I will not kill of you a parent nor an offspring.”

The fleas gather to the cane. You carry them — alive, every one, because killing any of them breaks the secret — and release them elsewhere. Sweep the house, recite the tawakkul verse forty times over it, and no flea ever enters again.

Frequently Asked Questions

  1. Why did the Prophet forbid cursing the flea?

    Two reports in the entry explain it: the Prophet heard a man cursing a flea and stopped him — “it woke a prophet for the Fajr prayer.” In 'Ali's version, fleas tormented the caravan at night and the Prophet called them “an excellent beast” that woke everyone for GOD's remembrance. Killing the flea is recommended, including for pilgrims; only the cursing is off-limits.

  2. What is the Persian-cane flea trick?

    A “proven secret” the book files as historical belief, not advice: smear a Persian cane with donkey's milk and goat fat, plant it mid-house, and adjure the fleas twenty-five times — as “a troop of GOD's troops” — to gather to it, promising to kill none. Carry them out alive, sweep, recite the tawakkul verse forty times — and the book claims no flea ever returns.

  3. Why does the book say a flea looks like an elephant?

    The comparison is about shape at a tiny scale: the entry describes the flea as having tusk-like biting parts and a trunk-like part for sucking blood. The article presents that image as part of the medieval book's account, not as modern zoological language.

Bird: Al-Bara (البرا) — the Bird Called Al-Samwil 🐦

Quick Answer

Al-barā (البرا) is a one-line entry in the medieval Arabic book Hayat al-Hayawan: a bird called al-samwil, read with a dammah on the bā'. The book gives no description at all — the full entry is promised later, in the sīn chapter. So this article is not about a bird. It is about one line, and what one line can teach us about how medieval scholars built a book.

The Whole Entry, Quoted Complete

Here it is — the entire entry, beginning to end:

بضم الباء طائر يسمى السمويل وسيأتي إن شاء الله تعالى في باب السين المهملة.

“In form: with a dammah on the bā' — a bird called al-samwil — and it will come, GOD willing, in the chapter of the unvoiced sīn.”

That is all the book says. No description of feathers or beak, no story, no remedy, no poem. But every word of that line is doing a job, so let us unpack it the way a classical reader would.

بضم الباء — “with a dammah on the bā'.” The word is read al-bu-rā, not al-ba-rā and not al-bi-rā. Arabic script leaves vowels unwritten, so a bare word on a page can be read three ways, and three readings can be three different words. Al-Damiri refuses to let you mispronounce his bird. It is the lexicographer's reflex: get the name right before anything else, because a mispronounced name is a lost animal.

طائر يسمى السمويل — “a bird called al-samwil.” The bird has another name, and the book hands it to you like a second key. Perhaps the reader knows the bird as al-samwil and not as al-barā — the entry is a signpost between two names, so whichever word you looked up, you land in the right place.

وسيأتي إن شاء الله تعالى في باب السين المهملة — “and it will come, GOD willing, in the chapter of the unvoiced sīn.” The full entry lives elsewhere, under al-samwil, in the sīn chapter — and he specifies the unvoiced sīn (المهملة, “the neglected one,” as opposed to the dotted shīn) the way a librarian specifies the shelf. The book's alphabetical order is its filing system, and al-Damiri is filing this bird where it belongs, not where you found it.

Coming Soon, Medieval Style

We think of “coming soon” as a modern tease — a trailer, a locked door on a website. The medieval version was this: a single line standing in an alphabetical chapter, pointing forward to another chapter. No apology for it. The reader of the 1300s was expected to wait.

And the waiting could be long. The book moves through its alphabet slowly — alif, bā', tā', and onward — and the sīn chapter sits deep inside it, entries and entries away. A reader who stumbled on البرا in the bā' chapter and wanted the full story of al-samwil had a journey ahead of him. This series follows the book in order, so the wait is ours too: the full entry arrives when we reach س, and not a page earlier.

There is something honest about the one-line entry. A lesser author would have padded it — copied a description from elsewhere, guessed, filled the space. Al-Damiri wrote one line and moved on. The book would rather say nothing than say something unverified. In a book of five hundred-plus entries, that restraint is a kind of integrity — and it is exactly why this series refuses to invent facts either.

The Honest Ledger

So here is the strict ledger, and I will not cross it. The entry confirms four things and nothing more: (1) the word is read al-barā, with a dammah on the bā'; (2) it is a bird; (3) the bird is also called al-samwil; (4) its full entry is deferred to the sīn chapter. That is the verified core. The book gives no description of the bird under this name — no habits, no stories, no remedies. What al-samwil looks like, where it lives, what it does: the book has not told us yet. When it does, in the sīn chapter, this series will be there — and we will meet the bird at last.

The Hoopoe (Al-Hudhud, الهدهد): Solomon's Spy With X-Ray Vision 🐦

Quick Answer

The hoopoe was Prophet Solomon's messenger bird — the one that discovered the Queen of Sheba and carried his letter to her. The medieval bestiary Hayat al-Hayawan claims this small bird could see water hidden deep beneath the earth, yet adds a humbling twist: a child could still trap it with a single grain, because “when destiny descends, sight is blinded.”

Of all the birds in this 700-year-old book, the hoopoe gets the most dramatic entrance. And honestly? It earns it.

The Bird That Went Missing

Picture the scene. Solomon — prophet and king — is reviewing his troops. Jinn, men, and birds, all lined up in formation. He scans the bird ranks.

One is missing.

“Why do I not see the hoopoe?” he demands. “Or is he among the absent? I will punish him severely — or slaughter him — unless he brings me a clear excuse.”

Not long after, the little bird comes fluttering back. And what he says next is pure courage — imagine a sparrow talking back to a king:

“I have learned something you do not know. I have come to you from Sheba with certain news.”

He had flown far beyond Solomon's kingdom and found a land ruled by a woman — a queen with a magnificent throne — whose people worshipped the sun instead of GOD. The hoopoe, this tiny bird, was offended. He couldn't let it stand. So he flew all the way back to report it.

Solomon's response? He doesn't punish the bird. He hands him a letter: “Take this to them. Deliver it, then watch what they do.”

The hoopoe became the mailman of a prophet. That letter led to the Queen of Sheba's famous visit — one of the great diplomatic encounters of the ancient world. All because a bird went AWOL and came back with gossip.

The Bird That Sees Underground Water

But here's the detail from Hayat al-Hayawan that stopped me cold.

The book claims the hoopoe can see water beneath the ground. When Solomon traveled through waterless desert, the hoopoe would spot hidden water from the sky and tell him where to dig. The jinn would then tear the earth open “as quickly as a sheep is skinned,” and the army would drink.

Think about that for a second. A bird with built-in groundwater radar. The medieval scholars were completely serious about this.

And then — this is my favorite part — the book immediately undercuts it with a story.

Ibn Abbas was once challenged: “You claim the hoopoe sees water deep in the earth — yet a mere boy can trap it! He puts a grain in a snare, covers it with dust, and the hoopoe walks right in!”

Ibn Abbas smiled and said something I've never forgotten: “Woe to you — when destiny descends, sight is blinded, and caution departs.”

Even X-ray vision can't save you from fate. What a line.

The Hoopoe's Strange Reputation

The entry is full of odd little details that paint a vivid picture:

His name was 'Anbar. At least, that's what the scholar al-Hasan reported as the personal name of Solomon's hoopoe. A bird with a first name.

His kunyas — the Arabs gave him honorific nicknames: Abu al-Akhbar (father of news — fitting for a messenger), Abu al-Rabi' (father of spring), and Abu Thumama.

His diet was... unfortunate. The book reports he eats blood, and his droppings smell foul. The medieval scholars didn't sugarcoat it — even a prophet's messenger had his gross habits.

His crest. Anyone who's seen a hoopoe knows that spectacular crown of feathers — orange-pink with black-and-white tips, fanning open like a royal headdress. The book treats it as a mark of his special status, and looking at the bird, you can see why the ancients thought so.

A small bird. A prophet's spy. A water-finder. And a reminder that no gift — however extraordinary — overrules destiny.

Frequently Asked Questions

  1. What is the hoopoe bird's role in the story of Solomon?

    In the Quran (Surah al-Naml), the hoopoe was absent from Solomon's bird review, then returned with news of Sheba — a kingdom ruled by a queen whose people worshipped the sun. Solomon sent the hoopoe back with a letter, making the bird the messenger in one of history's most famous diplomatic exchanges.

  2. Can hoopoes really see water underground?

    The medieval bestiary Hayat al-Hayawan claims the hoopoe could spot hidden groundwater, which Solomon's army used to find water in the desert. Modern science doesn't support X-ray vision in birds — but hoopoes do probe soil with their long bills for insects, which may have inspired the legend.

  3. What does “hudhud” mean and why is it spelled different ways?

    “Hudhud” (هدهد) is the Arabic name for the hoopoe, and “hoopoe” is its English name — both imitate the bird's distinctive “hoo-poo-poo” call. The Quran uses “al-hudhud,” which is why English-speaking Muslims often say “hudhud bird.”

Source: Al-Damiri, Hayat al-Hayawan al-Kubra, the hoopoe (الهدهد) entry. Quranic account: Surah al-Naml (27:20–28). Medieval beliefs are presented as historical curiosities, not facts.

📚 About the Book

A 700-year-old menagerie.

Hayat al-Hayawan al-Kubra (“The Great Life of Animals”) is an Arabic animal encyclopedia organized alphabetically by animal name. Its entries mix observations of nature with poetry, proverbs, religious traditions, medicine, dreams, history, and the meanings hidden inside words.

Its author, Kamal al-Din Muhammad al-Damiri (1341–1405), was an Egyptian scholar associated with Cairo. Trained in Islamic law and Arabic learning, he gathered material from hundreds of earlier works and shaped it into a vast reference book whose animals often open doors into human culture.

This website retells those old accounts for a modern audience while clearly treating medieval claims as historical lore—not established zoological fact.
